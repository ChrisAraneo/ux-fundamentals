# What is user experience?

## Definition

**User experience (UX) is how a person feels while using a product**: how easy it is, how pleasant it is, and whether it helps them get what they came for. A "product" can be almost anything: a mobile app, a website, a ticket machine, a kettle, or a service such as online banking.

UX design is the work of shaping that experience on purpose. A UX designer studies the people who will use a product, then makes decisions about its structure, behavior, and look so that using it feels natural instead of frustrating.

### A simple example

Imagine two coffee machines. On the first one, a single large button says "Coffee". On the second one, there are six unlabeled buttons and a blinking light that nobody understands. Both machines make the same coffee, but the experience of using them is completely different. UX design is about making products feel like the first machine.

## Why UX matters

Good UX matters to both users and businesses:

- **Users reach their goals faster** - they spend less time guessing what to do next and make fewer mistakes.
- **Users trust the product** - clear feedback (for example, a confirmation after a payment) makes people feel safe.
- **Businesses keep their customers** - people return to products that are pleasant to use and recommend them to others.
- **Teams save money** - problems found early during design are much cheaper to fix than problems found after launch.

## Qualities of a good user experience

A product with good UX is **usable, equitable, enjoyable, and useful**. These four qualities are a handy checklist for evaluating any design, including your own.

### Usable

A usable product has a **clear design, structure, and purpose**, so people can use it without effort. To check usability, ask questions such as: Can people quickly find what they need? Do they understand how each feature works? Can they finish their main task without help?

*Example:* in an online shop, clearly labeled "Add to cart" and "Remove from cart" buttons make shopping usable. In a train ticket app, buying a ticket for the next departure should take only a few taps from the home screen.

### Equitable

An equitable product is **helpful to people with different abilities and backgrounds**. It delivers a high-quality experience to everyone, regardless of age, gender, ethnicity, language, income, or disability. Equity goes further than giving everyone the same thing; it means giving each person the tools they need, because different people need different kinds of support. To check equity, ask: Did we consider a diverse group of users? Did we consider groups that products often overlook?

*Example:* a built-in translation feature, a choice of skin tones for emoji, or an app that works well with large system fonts.

### Enjoyable

An enjoyable product **creates a positive emotional connection** with the user. A product can work correctly without being enjoyable, but delight adds to an already functional product and makes people want to come back. To check enjoyment, ask: Does the design consider how users feel? Does anything make them smile? Does it keep them engaged?

*Example:* a language-learning app that celebrates a seven-day learning streak with a short, friendly animation.

### Useful

A useful product **solves a real problem** that the designer has identified. To check usefulness, ask: Does the design add value? Does it solve a user problem? Does it help users reach a specific goal?

*Example:* filter and sort options on a long list of products, or a budgeting app that warns you before your account balance gets too low.

### Usable vs. useful

These two words sound similar, but they mean different things:

- **Usable** - the product works well and is easy to use.
- **Useful** - the product solves a problem the user actually has.

A product can be one without being the other. A beautifully simple app that nobody needs is usable but not useful. A powerful tool hidden behind confusing menus is useful but not usable. Good UX aims for both.

## Good design prevents problems

Much of UX design is about **predicting where users might get stuck** and removing those obstacles before they happen. Good design is usually simple, well structured, and functional.

A typical example of bad design is a button that gives no feedback: the user taps "Send" and nothing visibly changes, so they don't know whether the message was sent. They might tap again and send it twice. A short "Message sent" confirmation solves the problem.

Another example is icons. Some icons, like a magnifying glass for search, are understood almost everywhere. Others are hard to guess. Adding a short text label under an unclear icon is a small change that makes the product easier for everyone.

::: tip Key takeaway
UX is about how people feel when they use a product. Aim for designs that are usable, equitable, enjoyable, and useful, and try to spot user roadblocks before users run into them.
:::
