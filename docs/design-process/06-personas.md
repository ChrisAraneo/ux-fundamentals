# Personas

## What is a persona?

A **persona** is a fictional user who represents a larger group of real users with similar goals, characteristics, and problems. You met personas briefly in [Chapter 1](../foundations/08-empathize-and-define). This page explains how to create them and why they are worth the effort.

Personas are fictional, but they are **not invented from nothing**. They are built from research: interviews, surveys, empathy maps, and the pain points you discovered. Each persona represents a **user group**, a set of people who share similar interests, goals, or concerns.

## Why use personas?

Personas are worth the time they take, for three main reasons:

1. **They build empathy** - a person with a name, a face, and a story is much easier to care about than a statistic. "Grace can't hear the machine's beep" sticks in your memory better than "12% of residents are hard of hearing".
2. **They tell stories** - personas turn a dry presentation into a story that stakeholders understand and remember, which makes it easier to win support for design decisions.
3. **They stress-test designs** - a solution that works for one persona might fail for another. Checking each idea against every persona helps you design for a wide range of people.

## What goes into a persona?

There is no single template, but most personas include:

- **Name and photo** - make the persona feel like a real person. Use an illustration or a stock photo that you have the rights to use.
- **Short biography** - age, occupation, education, where they live, family situation, and other details that matter for your product.
- **Goals** - what the persona wants to achieve, both in general and with your product.
- **Frustrations** - the pain points the persona experiences today.
- **Behaviors and skills** - for example, how comfortable they are with technology and which devices they use.
- **Accessibility needs** - any disability or limitation that affects how they use products.
- **Quote** - a short sentence in the persona's voice that sums up their attitude.

### Example: Grace

- **Biography** - Grace is 74, a retired librarian who lives alone on the fifth floor. She has two grown-up children who live in another city.
- **Goals** - to do her laundry once a week without stress and to stay independent.
- **Frustrations** - she can't hear the machine's end-of-cycle beep, so her clothes stay in the machine and neighbors complain. Carrying her basket down only to find all machines busy is tiring.
- **Behaviors and skills** - she uses a tablet with very large text for video calls with her family, but she distrusts apps that "need passwords for everything".
- **Accessibility needs** - she wears a hearing aid and needs large, high-contrast text.
- **Quote** - "I don't mind new technology, as long as it doesn't make me feel stupid."

Personas for Tomasz and Kofi would be built in the same way, each representing a different group of residents.

## How to create personas

1. **Look for patterns in your research** - review your empathy maps and notes, and group users who share similar goals, behaviors, and pain points.
2. **Define user groups** - give each group a short description, for example "parents who do laundry every day" or "residents who do laundry late at night".
3. **Create one persona per key group** - three to eight personas usually cover most of a product's users. You can't satisfy every individual need, so focus on the most important groups.
4. **Keep personas focused** - include only details that affect how people use your product. Grace's favorite food doesn't matter for WashSlot, but her hearing does.
5. **Share them with the team** - before creating personas, ask teammates who they think the users are. Afterward, compare their expectations with your research-based personas and point out where the data confirmed or contradicted them. A persona only helps if the whole team knows it well.

### Personas lead to design ideas

Personas often point directly to features. Because Grace can't hear the machine, WashSlot could send a **visual notification and a vibration** when her cycle ends. That same feature also helps Tomasz during work calls, which shows how designing for one persona can benefit others.

::: tip Key takeaway
Personas are research-based fictional characters that represent groups of real users. They build empathy, help you tell compelling stories, and let you test whether a design works for different people. Create one for each key user group and keep them focused on what matters for your product.
:::
