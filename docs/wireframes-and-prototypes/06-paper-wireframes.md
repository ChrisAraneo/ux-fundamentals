# Paper wireframes

## Why start on paper?

Drawing an existing app is good practice, but in real projects you draw wireframes for products or features that don't exist yet. At this stage, you want to explore many different ideas for the same screen, and paper is the best tool for that:

- **It's fast** - a few lines and boxes take seconds, much less time than building a screen in software.
- **It's cheap** - you only need paper and a pen.
- **It invites exploration** - because each sketch costs almost nothing, you can try many layouts, including unusual ones.
- **It doesn't need to be perfect** - paper wireframes are for getting ideas out, not for polishing details.

## Materials

You only need a sheet of plain paper (letter or A4 size, or larger) and a pencil or pen. A timer helps you keep each sketch short.

## How to create paper wireframes

### 1. Review your research and plans

Look again at your storyboards, user flow, and sitemap. They tell you which screens you need and what users do on each of them.

### 2. List the elements for the screen

Before drawing, write a short list of the information and controls that must appear on the screen. This list makes sure you don't forget anything in any version.

*WashSlot example:* the home screen needs a navigation menu, the app title, a profile avatar, the status of four machines, a "Book a slot" button, and a bottom navigation bar.

### 3. Draw at least five versions

Sketch at least five different ways to structure the same screen. Set a timer for about two minutes per version, so the whole exercise takes around ten minutes. Check your list as you draw, and don't be afraid of strange ideas; there is no single right answer.

*WashSlot example:* the designer sketches five versions of the home screen:

- **Version A** - machines shown as four large cards in a grid, each with a big status label.
- **Version B** - a simple list of machines with the "Book a slot" button fixed at the bottom.
- **Version C** - a picture of the laundry room seen from above, with each machine drawn in its real position.
- **Version D** - a timeline of the day showing when each machine is busy or free.
- **Version E** - the list from version B, plus a section at the top showing the user's next booking.

### 4. Mark the best parts

Review all versions and put a star next to the elements that work best. Ask yourself: What does the user need to do on this screen? Which element solves the problem statement most effectively?

*WashSlot example:* the designer stars the large, readable status labels from version A (good for Grace, who uses large text), the list and fixed button from version B (quick for Tomasz), and the "next booking" section from version E.

### 5. Combine them into a refined wireframe

Draw a new version that combines all starred elements. This becomes your first refined wireframe of the screen, for example "Home v1". Pick two or three of your strongest ideas to develop further digitally.

### 6. Repeat for the whole user flow

Repeat the process for every important screen until you have a complete user flow. Use your sitemap to see which screens you still need and to split the work into smaller parts. Aim for at least five key screens, and choose the ones that matter most for the main flow. An easy screen like "Contact us" may help you reach a number, but it may not be critical for users.

*WashSlot example:* after the home screen, the designer wireframes the booking screen, the summary, the confirmation, and "My bookings".

### 7. Take photos

Photograph or scan your paper wireframes. You will use them as a reference when you create digital wireframes, and they are excellent material for your portfolio, because they show your process.

## Creative ways to wireframe on paper

Plain paper works well, but there are other options:

- **Printed templates** - sheets with phone outlines already printed on them, so you don't have to draw the frame. Several screens fit on one page, which is handy for showing a flow.
- **Index cards** - draw one screen per card. You can rearrange cards on a table or wall, remove the ones you don't like, and combine cards from different teammates into one flow.
- **Sticky notes** - write one idea per note, or use different sizes and colors to mark different parts of the design.
- **Stencils** - plastic templates with common icons and shapes help your drawings look neat, which is useful if you feel unsure about drawing.
- **Demo device** - a cutout in the shape of a phone, into which you slide your sketches to see them at real size.

No method is better than the others. Choose what fits your situation: a demo device to check real-world size, index cards for team brainstorming, or a template for showing ideas to a stakeholder.

## Check your paper wireframes

When you're done, ask yourself whether you:

- **Prepared well** - gathered materials and listed the elements based on your storyboards and other research.
- **Explored many ideas** - drew at least five different versions of each important screen.
- **Followed the standards** - each wireframe shows the page structure, navigation, buttons, images, and text in the usual way.
- **Chose the best elements** - marked the most effective parts and combined them into a refined wireframe.
- **Completed the flow** - repeated the process for every screen the main user flow needs.

::: tip Key takeaway
Paper wireframes are the fastest and cheapest way to explore layouts. List the elements, sketch at least five versions of each screen, star the best parts, combine them into a refined wireframe, and repeat for the whole user flow. Keep photos of your sketches for your portfolio.
:::
