# Privacy and ethics

## Why privacy matters

Protecting participants' privacy is, first of all, simply the right thing to do. Researchers must act with integrity. There are also practical reasons:

- **Laws** - many countries regulate how personal data may be collected and stored. In the European Union, for example, the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) sets strict rules. Ignoring them can break the law.
- **Security** - poorly protected data can be stolen and published without consent.
- **Trust in the brand** - people who feel their data is safe trust the company more, even if they're research participants and not customers.

## What data needs protection?

There are two main types of personal data:

- **Personally identifiable information (PII)** - details that can identify a person, such as their name, home address, email address, or phone number.
- **Sensitive personally identifiable information (SPII)** - personal data whose loss or theft could lead to financial harm, embarrassment, or discrimination. Examples include ID and passport numbers, bank account numbers, date of birth, ethnicity, disability status, gender identity, sexual orientation, criminal history, and health information. Combined pieces of such data are especially dangerous.

## How to protect participants' data

1. **Be transparent** - tell participants exactly what data you're collecting.
2. **Collect only what you need** - if you don't need someone's date of birth, don't ask for it.
3. **Get active consent** - participants should actively agree to take part, usually by signing a consent form that explains how their information will be used and protected.
4. **Explain how you'll share their feedback** - for example, that quotes will be anonymized as "Participant 1", "Participant 2", and so on.
5. **Allow withdrawal at any time** - say clearly that participants can leave the study whenever they want, and make it easy: by email, by telling you, or just by not coming.
6. **Say who will have access** - for example, "Only our project team will see your feedback."
7. **Explain storage and deletion** - for example, "We'll keep the recordings in secure storage until the study ends and then delete them."

## Vulnerable groups

**Vulnerable groups** are people who have a limited ability to give consent or who have special privacy concerns. There's no fixed list, and vulnerability depends on the type of research and the culture. Examples can include children, people with some disabilities, older people, people in prison, and members of the LGBTQIA+ community. If you plan research with vulnerable participants, ask an experienced researcher or your organization's legal or ethics team which extra steps are needed. For example, children can only take part with their parents' or guardians' consent.

## Keeping research data safe

There are three main concerns about research data:

- **Data recording** - document the study and results consistently, following research standards. Consistent records make future studies comparable and help if there is an **audit**, an outside review that checks whether research involving people was ethical and followed its plan.
- **Data storage** - keep data safe from hacking and from physical damage, for example in secure, access-controlled storage.
- **Data retention** - decide how long data is kept. Some organizations limit how long records may be stored; others must keep them for a certain time by law. Also agree on who owns the data if you leave the company.

## Tools for protecting privacy

### De-identification

**De-identification** means removing information that could identify a participant. When sharing findings, refer to people as "Participant 1" instead of by name, or let participants choose a made-up name, which keeps the human touch. Also remove details that would make a person easy to recognize.

### Non-disclosure agreements

A **non-disclosure agreement (NDA)** protects your ideas. Participants in a study often see products before they're public, so there's a risk that ideas could be shared or copied. Asking participants to sign an NDA before the study gives you legal protection; if someone breaks the agreement, they may have to pay compensation.

## Ethics beyond the rules

Ethical research starts with the decision about whom you talk to. Many groups have historically been left out of research, so products often don't work well for them. Think about how you recruit, interview, interpret, and share information at every step. A simple test: **would you be comfortable if someone treated your data and your story this way?**

*WashSlot example:* the designer collects only first names and laundry habits, never asks about health conditions, refers to participants as P1 to P6 in all documents, stores recordings in the cooperative's secure drive, and deletes them a month after the study.

::: tip Key takeaway
Protect participants' privacy because it's right, legal, and builds trust. Collect only the data you need, get active consent, allow withdrawal, explain how data will be stored and deleted, take extra care with vulnerable groups, de-identify findings, and use NDAs to protect unreleased ideas.
:::
