# Homepage layouts

## Why the homepage layout matters

The **homepage** is the front door of a website. It's usually the first page people see, and it's the central point they return to when they want to go somewhere else. A **layout** is the structure that decides how the visual components on a page, such as images, text, and buttons, are arranged.

Homepages come in many forms, but a handful of layouts appear again and again. Because people have seen them many times, these layouts help visitors understand a page quickly. Knowing them gives you a vocabulary for discussing ideas and a set of starting points for your own sketches.

## Common layouts

### Single-column layout

A **single-column layout** puts all content in one vertical column: usually navigation at the top, the content in the middle, and a footer at the bottom. It's the simplest layout, it's ideal for reading, and it's the standard choice for phone screens, where there's no room for columns side by side. On a large screen, it works well when one thing deserves all the attention, such as a long article, a video, or a sign-up form.

### Multicolumn layout

A **multicolumn layout** uses two or more columns. It's common on desktop websites because it makes good use of wide screens and fits more content **above the fold**, the part of the page that's visible without scrolling.

A good multicolumn page has a clear **focal point**, a distinct area that catches the eye first, such as a large featured story. Supporting content usually takes up a smaller share of the page, roughly a quarter to a half. When there are many columns, use visual weight, hierarchy, and color to show what matters most. On news and shopping websites, the narrow column at the far left or right is often used for ads.

### Box layout

A **box layout** arranges content in boxes of different sizes and proportions, often on the homepage. Each box is an independent item: a category, a promotion, or a project. Equal boxes with equal spacing look equally important, while a larger or separated box draws attention. Online stores like this layout because they can swap boxes for seasonal offers easily, and many designers use it in portfolios to show one project per box.

### Featured image layout

A **featured image layout** (also called a **single image layout**) fills most of the screen with one large image or video. It creates a strong first impression and an emotional connection, so it's popular for travel, fashion, and product websites. The image must be high quality and closely related to the website's message. This layout often comes with a **sticky** (fixed) navigation bar, which stays at the top of the screen while the user scrolls.

### Asymmetrical layouts

An **asymmetrical layout** is intentionally unbalanced: one side of the page carries more weight than the other, which draws attention to certain images or headlines. Asymmetrical layouts rely on **eye-tracking patterns**, the paths that people's eyes typically follow across a page.

- **F-shaped pattern** - people read across the top, then across a little lower, then scan down the left side. This suits text-heavy pages and search results. Place the key information first, and use clear subheadings on the left, because many people never read to the bottom.
- **Z-shaped pattern** - people scan from top left to top right, then diagonally down to the left, then across to the right again. This suits pages with little text and a clear call to action at the end of the "Z". It follows the natural reading direction of left-to-right languages; for right-to-left languages, the pattern is mirrored.

People who use assistive technology don't scan visually, but a well-ordered page lets their screen reader follow the same logical order from most to least important.

### Grid of cards layout

This layout is built from **cards**, small rectangles that each preview more detailed content, such as a video, a recipe, or an article. The cards are usually the same size. Video platforms, recipe blogs, and image boards use this layout.

It may look like a box layout, but there's a key difference. In a box layout, each box stands on its own. In a grid of cards, the cards belong to one list of similar items, often generated automatically, such as search results. Grids of cards can often switch to a list view, and many load more cards as the user scrolls (a technique called **lazy loading**). Because the number of columns can change with the screen size, cards work very well in responsive design.

### Tiered layer cake layout

The **tiered layer cake layout** stacks horizontal rows, or layers, on top of each other. Each layer can have a different number of columns, such as one wide image, then three columns of text, then two columns of images. Unlike a classic multicolumn layout, there is no long sidebar running down the whole page. Because each layer can be rearranged or reduced to one column on its own, this layout adapts easily to different screen sizes.

## Choosing a layout

Web design trends change quickly, and a fashionable layout isn't necessarily usable or accessible. Choose the layout that best supports your users and your content:

- **Start from the main task** - what do most visitors want to do or find first? Make that the focal point.
- **Consider the amount of content** - lots of text suggests an F-shaped or single-column layout; little text and one goal suggest a Z-shaped or featured image layout.
- **Think about all screen sizes** - layouts made of cards, boxes, or layers adapt more easily than complex multicolumn grids.
- **Combine layouts** - one homepage can mix several, for example a featured image at the top and a grid of cards below.

*WashSlot example:* the designer compares three options for the homepage.

1. **Featured image** - a large photo of the bright, renovated laundry room. It looks friendly, but it pushes the machine status below the fold, and residents come to the website to check status, not to admire the room. Rejected.
2. **Grid of cards** - the four washing machines and two dryers as six equal cards. This makes the status easy to scan and adapts well to small screens. Kept for the status section.
3. **Tiered layer cake** - three layers: the machine cards at the top, then two columns with "Your next booking" and a large "Book a slot" button, then a row of the three latest notices. Chosen as the overall layout, with the grid of cards as its first layer.

For the "House rules" page, which is mostly text, the designer uses an F-shaped layout: a short summary at the top and bold subheadings down the left side, such as "Quiet hours", "Collecting your laundry", and "Cleaning the filters".

::: tip Key takeaway
Common homepage layouts include single-column, multicolumn, box, featured image, asymmetrical (F- and Z-shaped), grid of cards, and tiered layer cake. Familiar layouts help visitors understand a page quickly. Choose based on your users' main task and your content, not on trends, and remember that one page can combine several layouts.
:::
