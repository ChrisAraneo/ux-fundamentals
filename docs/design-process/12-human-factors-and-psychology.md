# Human factors and psychology

## What are human factors?

**Human factors** are all the human characteristics, abilities, and limitations that people bring to their interactions with products. Designing with human factors in mind means adapting the product to people, not expecting people to adapt to the product.

This idea is younger than you might think. For a long time, people were trained to fit machines. In the first half of the 20th century, especially in aviation, it became clear that machines had to be designed to fit the people using them, because not every pilot could be an expert. Cockpits were redesigned so that pilots with different skill levels could fly safely. This work grew into the field of human factors and ergonomics, which strongly influenced UX design.

## Common human factors

Some of the most common human factors designers consider are:

- **Impatience** - people want results quickly and leave slow or complicated products.
- **Limited memory** - people can't remember many details at once, such as codes or long instructions.
- **Limited attention** - people get distracted and often skim instead of reading.
- **Need for familiar comparisons** - new things are easier to understand when they resemble something people already know.
- **Need for motivation** - people need a reason to start and continue a task.
- **Changing needs** - what people need can change over time or with their situation.
- **Fears and prejudices** - people bring worries and beliefs that shape how they react.
- **Errors and misjudgment** - everyone makes mistakes, so products must help prevent and fix them.

A well-known example is the short summary at the start of a long message, often marked "TL;DR" ("too long; didn't read"). It respects readers' impatience, limited attention, and limited memory by giving them the key points first.

Human factors aren't only limitations. They also create opportunities. For example, some brands bring back their old packaging designs to evoke **nostalgia**, a warm feeling of the past that connects people emotionally to the product.

## Psychological principles in UX

### Mental models

A **mental model** is a person's internal picture of how something works, based on experience. It lets people predict what will happen when they act. When you see a door, your mental model says that opening it lets you pass through. If you opened a door and found a brick wall behind it, your mental model would break.

In UX, products should match users' mental models. People expect a shopping cart icon to lead to their cart and a magnifying glass to start a search. *WashSlot example:* residents already know booking calendars from doctors' offices and hairdressers, so WashSlot shows machine slots in a similar familiar calendar view.

### Feedback loops

A **feedback loop** is the response a user gets after an action, which tells them whether it worked. When you press a light switch, the light turns on (positive feedback) or nothing happens (negative feedback). Over time, feedback teaches users what to expect.

Every action in a product should get clear feedback. *WashSlot example:* after booking a slot, the app shows a confirmation screen, vibrates, and adds the booking to the user's list, so nobody wonders whether the booking was saved.

### Von Restorff effect

The **Von Restorff effect**, also called the isolation effect, means that when several similar items are shown together, people remember the one that looks different. That's why the most important button on a screen, the **call to action (CTA)**, usually has a different color or shape from the other buttons.

*WashSlot example:* on the machine list, the "Book" button is the only element in a bright color, so users notice it immediately.

### Serial position effect

The **serial position effect** means that people best remember the first and last items in a list, while items in the middle tend to blur. That's why many apps and websites place the most important navigation items at the beginning and end of a menu bar.

*WashSlot example:* the bottom navigation starts with "Machines" and ends with "My bookings", the two most-used sections.

### Hick's law

**Hick's law** says that the more options people have, the longer it takes them to decide. A menu with five clear choices is faster to use than one with twenty. Offering many options can seem generous, but it often makes decisions harder.

*WashSlot example:* instead of showing every 15-minute slot of the day, the app first suggests the three nearest free slots, with "Show more" for users who want to choose themselves.

## Use psychology ethically

Psychological principles are powerful, so use them responsibly. The goal is to **help** users make good decisions, not to trick them into choices that benefit only the business, such as hidden subscriptions or fake countdown timers. Use psychology to empower users, not to manipulate them.

::: tip Key takeaway
Human factors are the abilities and limitations people bring to every product. Design with them in mind, match users' mental models, give clear feedback, and use principles such as the Von Restorff effect, the serial position effect, and Hick's law ethically, to help users rather than manipulate them.
:::
