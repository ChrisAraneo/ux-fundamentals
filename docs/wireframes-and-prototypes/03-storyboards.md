# Storyboards

## What is a storyboard?

A **storyboard** is a series of drawn panels that shows a user's experience with a product, step by step, like a comic strip. Film directors use storyboards to plan scenes before shooting. UX designers use them to imagine and explain how a product fits into a person's life and how it solves their problem.

Storyboards connect your research insights with the flow of the experience. They help you think the experience through, and they help stakeholders quickly understand your idea. A real product may have dozens of screens, but a storyboard shows only the most important moments.

## The four elements of a storyboard

Every storyboard tells a story, and every story needs four elements:

1. **Character** - the user the story is about, usually one of your personas.
2. **Scene** - the user's environment: where they are and what is happening around them.
3. **Plot** - the benefit or solution your design offers.
4. **Narrative** - the user's problem and how the design solves it, from beginning to end.

## The storyboard template

Storyboards are usually drawn on a simple template:

- **Scenario** - one short sentence at the top that describes the user and the problem the design solves.
- **Panels** - usually six boxes with simple sketches. You don't need artistic skills; stick figures and basic shapes are enough.
- **Captions** - short descriptions under each panel. They are especially helpful for moments that are hard to draw.

## Two types of storyboards

| | Big-picture storyboard | Close-up storyboard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Focus** | The user and their experience | The product and its screens |
| **Answers** | How and why will people use the product? | What happens on each screen? |
| **Shows** | Context, environment, emotions, pain points | Screens, actions, and transitions |
| **Best used** | Early, to explain ideas and win stakeholder support | Later, after the first design directions are chosen |

You can create both types for the same project. You can also mix them, for example by starting with the user's situation and then zooming in on the screens. Be flexible.

## How to create a big-picture storyboard

1. **Begin from your problem statement** - it gives you the character and the scene.
2. **Add your goal statement** - it gives you the plot, the benefit your design offers.
3. **Set up the template** - draw six panels or use a printed template.
4. **Write the scenario** - one sentence about who the user is and which problem the design removes for them.
5. **Give each panel one idea** - open with the setting, and let every following panel move the story forward.
6. **Show emotions** - big-picture storyboards are about people, so draw faces that show how the user feels at each moment.
7. **Expose pain points** - show the moments of frustration the design must solve.
8. **End with the goal** - the last panel shows the user reaching their goal, and how good that feels.

### Example: Tomasz's big-picture storyboard

**Scenario:** *An app that shows which laundry machines are free and lets residents book them in advance.*

1. **Wasted trip** - Tomasz carries a heavy basket to the basement. Every machine is busy. He looks frustrated.
2. **Interrupted work** - back at his desk, he is on a video call, but keeps thinking about the laundry.
3. **Discovering WashSlot** - he reads about WashSlot in the building newsletter and opens the app on his phone.
4. **Booking** - he sees that machine 3 is free at 17:30 and books it with two taps. He smiles.
5. **Reminder** - during another call, his phone vibrates quietly: "Your laundry is ready in 10 minutes."
6. **Goal reached** - Tomasz collects the laundry with his kids, without a single wasted trip. Everyone is happy.

## How to create a close-up storyboard

The first four steps are the same: problem statement, goal statement, template, and scenario. The difference is in the panels. A close-up storyboard puts the **product** at the center: what the user sees on each screen, which buttons they press, and how they move from one screen to the next. It asks questions such as:

- **Actions** - what does the user need to do or decide on each screen?
- **Buttons** - which controls are visible on each screen?
- **Transitions** - what takes the user from one screen to the next?
- **Assumptions** - what are we assuming about this experience?
- **Risks** - which interactions might be complicated or cause problems?

You don't need to show every click; pick the key screens.

### Example: Tomasz's close-up storyboard

1. **Open** - a hand taps the WashSlot icon on a phone's home screen.
2. **Home** - the screen lists four machines; machine 3 shows a green "Free" label.
3. **Choose a time** - a list of time slots with "17:30" highlighted.
4. **Summary** - booking details and a switch labeled "Remind me 10 minutes before the end".
5. **Confirmation** - a check mark and the text "Machine 3 is yours at 17:30".
6. **Alert** - a notification on the lock screen: "Your laundry is ready in 10 minutes".

Notice the difference: the big-picture storyboard shows Tomasz's feelings and life, while the close-up storyboard shows the app. You will use the close-up sketches later as a starting point for [wireframes](./04-fidelity-and-wireframes).

::: tip Key takeaway
A storyboard tells a user's story in a series of panels, built from four elements: character, scene, plot, and narrative. Big-picture storyboards focus on the user's experience and emotions; close-up storyboards show the product's screens and actions.
:::
