# Design critiques

## Why feedback matters

**Feedback** means asking for, or receiving, other people's ideas about what is and isn't working in your design. Good feedback raises questions and ideas you hadn't thought of. Designers ask for it at every stage of a project, not just at the end, because:

- **It improves your designs** - teammates spot problems and opportunities you missed.
- **It builds your skills and confidence** - regular feedback shows you how much you've grown over time.
- **It widens your perspective** - people with different skills and backgrounds notice needs you didn't imagine, which also helps reduce the effect of your own biases.

Receiving feedback can be uncomfortable, especially at first. It helps to remember that people are commenting on the design, not on you. With practice, you'll learn that you don't have to follow every suggestion, and your confidence in your own decisions will grow. Check yourself from time to time: are you asking teammates for feedback regularly, and are you comfortable sharing your own opinions about their work?

## How to give useful feedback

1. **Adapt it to the person** - consider their role (designer, engineer, or product manager) and their experience level.
2. **Give a reason** - "I don't like this color" doesn't help anyone. "This color may not have enough contrast for the button text" explains the problem and makes it actionable.
3. **Describe problems, not solutions** - point out what isn't working and why, and let the designer find the fix. They know the project best, and they'll learn more by solving it.
4. **Connect it to the goals** - link your comment to the users, the project's goals, or a design principle, so it's more than a personal taste.
5. **Choose the right moment** - once your feedback and reasoning are clear, find a suitable time and place to share it.

## What is a design critique?

A **design critique session** (often shortened to **crit**) is a planned meeting in which designers present their work and listen to feedback from teammates. It's a chance for the group to exchange ideas and agree on a design direction, in person or online. A well-prepared critique isn't a trial; it's a working session that improves the design and helps the team work together.

### Roles in a critique

- **Facilitator** - runs the session: sets the agenda and the goals, keeps time, keeps the discussion on topic, and makes sure everyone gets a chance to speak, including quieter people. Small critiques sometimes run without one.
- **Presenter** - the designer (or group of designers) sharing the work. They prepare the presentation, listen actively, and ask clarifying questions.
- **Reviewers** - the people who give feedback on the design and suggest what needs to change. There are usually several.
- **Notetaker** - an optional role. They record all feedback, often in a shared document, so the presenter can concentrate on the conversation.

Over your career, you'll play all of these roles.

### How a critique usually runs

1. **The facilitator opens** - introduces the session and its objectives. Good objectives name a clear topic and a deadline, such as "Get feedback on the phone navigation before we build the high-fidelity prototype on Friday."
2. **The presenter shares the work** - while reviewers note questions, things they like, and concerns.
3. **Reviewers give feedback** - one at a time, explaining their reasoning. The presenter asks clarifying questions and responds thoughtfully.
4. **The facilitator closes** - raises any questions related to the objectives that haven't been covered and summarizes the next steps.

A typical critique lasts at least 30 minutes, and the presentation usually takes only 5 to 10 of them. The rest is discussion.

## Presenting your work

Preparation is the presenter's job. Be ready to explain the thinking behind your decisions by answering five questions:

1. **Who are the users?**
2. **Which problem does the design address?**
3. **How well does it address the problem so far, and where does it fall short?**
4. **Which stage of the process have you reached?**
5. **Which parts do you want comments on?**

The last question keeps the discussion focused, which is one of the hardest parts of presenting: reviewers naturally drift to other parts of the product. Give reviewers enough context, but keep the presentation short. If your prototype is complex, send it to reviewers before the session.

During the session, **listen actively**. Don't interrupt, and don't defend every decision. Ask questions until you understand each reviewer's point of view. You'll have time afterward to decide what to do.

## From feedback to action

After the critique, review the notes and decide what to act on. Think of yourself as the captain of a ship: the feedback tells you whether you're heading in the right direction or need to adjust your course a little or a lot, but the decision is yours.

1. **Review all notes** - go through every comment, so nothing gets lost.
2. **Synthesize** - with many reviewers, group similar comments into themes, as you did with usability study notes in [Chapter 4](../research-and-testing/12-from-observations-to-insights).
3. **Decide what to act on** - prioritize feedback about function, the user experience, accessibility, and inconsistencies. Comments based only on personal taste, with no reasoning, are usually "nice to have".
4. **Check your own motives** - when you reject a suggestion, ask whether it's because your idea truly fits users better or just because you're attached to it.
5. **Involve others when needed** - some feedback affects more than the design, such as the business, the budget, or the technology. Turn it into a conversation with the product manager or the relevant team.
6. **Write action items** - turn each accepted comment into a concrete task, and note follow-up questions for reviewers.
7. **Iterate** - make the changes, and be ready to explain your choices the next time you share the design.

*WashSlot example:* the designer presents the WashSlot mockups at a monthly online critique run by a local group of UX designers. The facilitator states the objective: "Feedback on the phone navigation and the booking page, in 30 minutes." Two designers review, and a third takes notes. Afterward, the designer sorts the feedback:

| Feedback | Decision |
| --- | --- |
| "I'd use a warmer color than teal." | No action: a personal preference with no reason given, and the teal matches the app. |
| "On the phone, 'Report a problem' is hidden in the menu, but a broken machine is urgent." | Action: add a "Report" link to each machine card that's out of order. |
| "The shared tablet stays logged in. Could the next person see or cancel someone else's bookings?" | Action: an edge case that affects privacy and the budget, so the designer emails the board to agree on an automatic logout. |
| "The time buttons look different widths on the tablet." | Action: check all time buttons and apply the same component and width everywhere. |
| "The carousel arrows look faint on pale teal." | Action: check the contrast. The arrows measure 2.4:1, below the 3:1 minimum for graphics, so they're darkened to deep teal. |

::: tip Key takeaway
Feedback improves designs, skills, and perspective. Give feedback that fits the person, comes with a reason, and describes problems rather than solutions. In a design critique, the facilitator runs the session, the presenter shares and listens, reviewers give feedback, and a notetaker may record it. Prepare with the five questions, listen actively, then synthesize the feedback, prioritize function and accessibility, and turn it into concrete action items.
:::
