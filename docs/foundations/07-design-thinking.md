# Design thinking framework

## What is a framework?

A **framework** is a structure that guides you through solving a problem. It describes the steps to follow and the good practices to use, so you don't have to start from zero each time. In UX, frameworks help teams build solutions to the problems of real users.

## What is design thinking?

**Design thinking** is one of the most popular UX frameworks. It solves problems by starting from people's needs and combines research, idea generation, prototyping, and testing. It helps you understand who your users are, what problems they have, and what your design should include.

Design thinking has five phases:

1. **Empathize** - learn about your users, their needs, and the context in which they will use your product.
2. **Define** - decide which user problem is the most important one to solve, and describe it clearly.
3. **Ideate** - come up with as many possible solutions as you can, then choose the most promising ones.
4. **Prototype** - build early models of the solution that you can show to users.
5. **Test** - try the prototypes with users and learn what works and what doesn't.

### Design thinking is not a straight line

The phases are listed in order, but in practice **design thinking is iterative**. You will often go back to an earlier phase. For example, testing might show that you misunderstood a problem, so you return to the empathize or define phase and try again.

You can learn about each phase in detail on the next pages: [empathize and define](./08-empathize-and-define), [ideate](./09-ideate), and [prototype and test](./10-prototype-and-test).

## Principles shared by all UX frameworks

Design thinking is only one of many frameworks. Teams choose a framework based on the product and the organization. Whatever framework you use, the core principles stay the same:

- **Focus on the user** - every decision should start from real user needs, not from assumptions.
- **Solve real problems** - create solutions that address the problems users actually have.
- **Collaborate across teams** - work closely with engineers, product managers, marketing, and other departments.
- **Validate your designs** - check your ideas with real users instead of trusting your own opinion.
- **Iterate** - improve the design step by step until the experience is right.

## Business requirements

Design thinking officially starts with empathizing, but **real projects usually start with business requirements**. Business requirements come from whoever orders the project: a company, a nonprofit organization, a public institution, or a private client. They usually contain two kinds of information: goals and parameters.

### Business goals

Business goals describe what the client wants to achieve:

- **Target users** - the people the design should serve.
- **Expected outcomes** - what the design should achieve, for example more online orders or fewer support calls.
- **Vision** - how the client imagines the final look and functionality.

### Business parameters

Business parameters describe the limits the design has to fit into:

- **Budget, timeline, and scope** - how much money and time are available and how big the project is.
- **Tools and systems** - the software, platforms, or existing systems the design must use or work with.
- **Standards** - legal, technical, or brand requirements the design must meet, such as accessibility regulations.

Business requirements can change during a project and are often negotiated. Good UX designers **keep them in mind in every phase**. For example, user research takes time and money, so it must be planned to fit the budget and schedule.

## Running example: the city library

The next pages follow one fictional project from start to finish. Here are its business requirements.

A small **city library** wants a better way for members to reserve books. Today, members can reserve books only by phone or at the front desk during opening hours (9:00 to 17:00). Staff write reservations on paper, and some of them get lost. The library hires a freelance UX designer to solve this.

The library's goals and parameters are:

- **Goal** - let members reserve books online and pick them up from a new self-service locker that is available 24 hours a day.
- **Target users** - the library believes most of its members are students and retirees.
- **Budget and time** - a small budget and three months to launch.
- **Systems** - the solution must connect to the library's existing book catalog.
- **Standards** - as a public institution, the library must meet accessibility regulations and follow the city's visual identity.

These requirements give the designer a starting point: a first idea of the users, the budget, the timeline, and what success looks like.

::: tip Key takeaway
Design thinking is a five-phase, user-centered framework: empathize, define, ideate, prototype, and test. Real projects begin with business requirements (goals and parameters), and good designers respect them in every phase.
:::
