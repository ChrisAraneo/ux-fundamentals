# From ideas to prototypes

## Where are we in the process?

You have empathized with users, defined their problems, and generated ideas. Now you are moving into the **prototype** phase of design thinking. In this phase, ideas take shape: first as rough sketches, then as clickable models that people can try.

As you learned in [Chapter 1](../foundations/10-prototype-and-test), a **prototype** is a preliminary version of a product that demonstrates how it will work, long before the real thing is built. Users and stakeholders can interact with it and give feedback before the team spends a lot of time and money.

## What does a prototype show?

A prototype helps everyone visualize interactive design work. It shows:

- **What the product does** - what happens at each moment of the experience.
- **What is interactive** - which elements users can tap, type into, or drag.
- **How the product will work** - how it should behave once it's real.

A prototype can cover a whole product or just one small interaction, such as a booking form.

## Why build prototypes?

Prototypes help UX designers:

- **Make ideas real** - an abstract concept becomes something people can see and use.
- **Think through problems** - building the flow reveals gaps you didn't notice on paper.
- **Iterate** - you can improve the design quickly based on research and feedback.
- **Find problems early** - usability issues and unexpected situations show up before development.
- **Communicate** - stakeholders and engineers understand a prototype much faster than a written description.
- **Test before launch** - you can learn from real users before any code is written.

Choose the level of detail based on what you want to learn. A rough paper prototype is enough to test whether users understand the basic flow. A detailed prototype is better for testing specific interactions or convincing stakeholders.

## The path from research to prototype

Most teams follow a similar sequence of activities. You will learn each of them in this chapter.

### 1. Align research with project goals

Before drawing anything, look at your research again and connect it to the project goals. Useful questions are:

- **Goals** - what is the project trying to achieve?
- **Competition** - which products do users already use?
- **Audience** - who are the users, and what do they want to accomplish?
- **Platform** - what kind of product is it, and on which devices will it run?
- **Precedents** - are there visual styles or existing products to follow?
- **Deliverables** - what exactly does the team need to produce?

### 2. Create low-fidelity wireframes

Next, sketch the structure of the product with simple tools such as paper and pencil or a whiteboard:

1. **Map the user flows** - draw how users move through the product to reach their goals (see [user flows](./02-user-flows)).
2. **Define inputs and outputs** - decide what information users enter, what the product shows them in return, and how this matches their expectations.
3. **Sketch the first wireframes** - draw the screens from your user flows, focusing on structure and function.
4. **Add content structure** - mark where text, photos, or videos will go, using simple boxes and lines instead of real content.

Remember that **wireframes and prototypes are not the same**. A wireframe is a static picture of a screen. It is quick and cheap to make and great for team discussions, but you can't interact with it. A wireframe is the first step toward a prototype.

### 3. Create more detailed wireframes

When the structure is clear, the designer recreates the wireframes digitally in a design tool such as Figma. Digital wireframes are cleaner and more precise, and they can gradually gain more detail.

### 4. Create a clickable prototype

The designer then connects the screens, so that tapping a button opens the next screen. This **clickable prototype** lets people move through the product and experience the flow.

### 5. Create a functional prototype

Later, a **functional prototype** adds richer behavior: animations, small interactions (such as a switch that slides or a list item that can be dragged), and realistic content. It looks and behaves as close to the final product as possible. You will build detailed prototypes in later chapters.

## Prototypes are drafts

It's easy to forget, even for experienced teams, that a prototype is not the final product. It is one version in a series of iterations, made to learn something. Expect to change it, sometimes completely, after you show it to users.

::: tip Key takeaway
A prototype is an early, interactive model of a product used to explore ideas and gather feedback. The usual path is: align research with goals, map user flows, sketch low-fidelity wireframes, create digital wireframes, and connect them into a clickable prototype.
:::
