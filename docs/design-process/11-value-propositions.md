# Value propositions

## What is a value proposition?

A **value proposition** explains why someone should use your product or service instead of another one, or instead of doing nothing at all. It's the reason users will be interested in your product and willing to try it.

Everything your product offers might seem obvious to you, because you know it well. Users don't. A value proposition makes the value clear to them in a few words.

## Two key questions

Every value proposition answers two questions:

1. **What does the product do?** - describe clearly what the product offers or lets users do.
2. **Why should users care?** - explain how it solves users' pain points, ideally in a way no other product does.

## How to find your value propositions

### Step 1: List all features and benefits

Write down every feature and benefit you can think of, big and small. Don't filter yet; you will narrow the list later.

*WashSlot example:* live machine status, booking slots, end-of-cycle alerts with vibration, reminders before a slot starts, house rules in several languages, a tablet in the laundry room for residents without smartphones, reporting broken machines, statistics about the busiest hours, a chat for neighbors, a dark theme, a points system for punctual residents, and paying for cycles in the app.

### Step 2: Group them by value

Sort the list into a few categories of value that came up in your research. Set aside items that don't fit any category or don't bring real value to users.

*WashSlot example:* the research showed four values that matter to residents: **saving time**, **fairness**, **inclusion**, and **reliability**. The chat for neighbors, the points system, and the dark theme don't fit these categories, so the team sets them aside for now.

### Step 3: Match value to personas

Pair each persona with the value that solves their biggest pain point. This separates truly valuable features from "nice ideas" that nobody asked for.

*WashSlot example:*

- **Tomasz** - live machine status (saving time).
- **Grace** - end-of-cycle alerts with vibration and the laundry room tablet (inclusion).
- **Kofi** - late-night booking and house rules in several languages (fairness and inclusion).

### Step 4: Find what makes you unique

Review the list and remove the value propositions that competitors already offer. What remains is your **unique value proposition**, the thing that makes your product stand out.

One practical way to find gaps is to read competitors' reviews, starting with the lowest ratings. Repeated complaints show needs that nobody is meeting yet. You will learn a structured way to analyze competitors on the [competitive audits](./14-competitive-audits) page.

*WashSlot example:* several existing laundry apps show machine status, but reviews often complain that they are "useless for people without a smartphone" and "only in one language". Inclusion becomes WashSlot's unique value proposition.

## Writing the value proposition

Combine your answers into one or two short sentences. For example:

> WashSlot shows which laundry machines are free and lets every resident book them, by phone, tablet, or in the laundry room itself, in their own language. No more wasted trips and no more arguments over forgotten laundry.

A good value proposition is **short, clear, and convincing**. It speaks to users' needs and shows what makes the product different. Sometimes it even shows users a need they didn't realize they had, and that is often where innovation happens.

::: tip Key takeaway
A value proposition tells users what your product does and why they should care. List all features, group them by the values users care about, match them to personas, and remove what competitors already offer to find your unique value.
:::
