# Bias in usability studies

## Everyone brings bias into a study

A **bias** is a tendency to favor or reject something based on limited information. You learned about biases in research in [Chapter 1](../foundations/13-bias-in-research) and about biases in design in [Chapter 3](../wireframes-and-prototypes/11-bias-in-design). Usability studies are especially sensitive to bias, because two people, the moderator and the participant, influence each other in real time. Nobody can be completely unbiased, but you can learn to notice biases and limit their effect.

## Biases to watch for

### Implicit bias

**Implicit bias** is the set of unconscious attitudes and stereotypes we connect with groups of people. A moderator might expect an older participant to struggle with an app, or a young participant to breeze through it, and then interpret their behavior through that expectation.

### Confirmation bias

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to notice information that supports what you already believe. If you're convinced your booking screen is clear, you might overlook the moments when participants hesitate.

To reduce it, try these strategies:

- **Recruit enough participants** - five to eight people with different perspectives make it harder for one belief to dominate.
- **Welcome contradictions** - when participants surprise you, dig deeper instead of dismissing their feedback.

### Serial position effect

The **serial position effect** means people recall the beginning and the end of a list more easily than its middle. It affects both sides: you may remember the start and end of a session more clearly, and participants may remember only the first and last steps of your instructions. Give instructions one at a time, and take notes during the whole session.

### Leading questions

**Leading questions** are worded in a way that pushes participants toward a certain answer. Compare "How did you find the machine you wanted?" with "Was the big blue button easy to find?" The second question suggests that the button is easy to find and steers the participant toward agreeing.

To reduce it, try these strategies:

- **Encourage thinking aloud** - when participants describe their own process, you hear their experience, not the one you hoped for. Pause after each task to give them extra time.
- **Limit your reactions** - avoid comments like "Yes, exactly!", which encourage participants to look for more approval.

### Friendliness bias

**Friendliness bias** is the tendency to agree with people we like, to keep the conversation pleasant. If you build a very warm rapport, participants may avoid saying anything negative. Agreeing is also the easiest path, which is why this tendency is linked to the "principle of least effort".

To reduce it, try these strategies:

- **Ask for honesty openly** - say at the start that you need honest, critical feedback and that it won't hurt your feelings.
- **Stay equally engaged** - show the same curiosity and attention whether the feedback is positive or negative.

### Social desirability bias

**Social desirability bias** is the tendency to answer in a way that makes you look good to others. Participants may praise the product or hide their difficulties to avoid looking incompetent.

To reduce it, try these strategies:

- **Interview people one at a time** - this bias is stronger in groups, where people want to look good in front of others.
- **Guarantee confidentiality** - tell participants that their names won't be published and that negative feedback won't be traced back to them.
- **Offer statements to choose from** - show a few different statements, positive and negative, and ask which one the participant relates to most, stressing that there's no correct answer.

### The Hawthorne effect

The **Hawthorne effect** is the tendency of people to behave differently when they know they're being observed, for example trying harder than they normally would. Knowing that a session is recorded or that stakeholders are watching can change behavior.

To reduce it, try these strategies:

- **Create a relaxed environment** - give participants time to settle in before starting the tasks, and remind them there are no wrong answers.
- **Build rapport** - make small talk and use the participant's name, so the session feels personal rather than like an experiment.

## Strategies for reducing your own bias

1. **Identify your biases** - before designing or testing, reflect on your assumptions. Try a quick exercise: write down who you imagine using a product and how. For example, for a cooking app, who did you picture: a woman, a man, a student, a grandparent, someone who cooks every day, someone who never cooks? Every answer you didn't think of reveals an assumption.
2. **Recruit a representative sample** - include your key user groups and groups that are often marginalized.
3. **Set the research criteria in advance** - agree on research goals and questions with your team before the study, so you don't change direction to fit your expectations.
4. **Word questions carefully** - use neutral, open questions such as "How did it go?" or "What worked and what didn't, and why?"
5. **Let participants express themselves fully** - give them time, and ask follow-up questions to understand exactly how they feel and why.
6. **Watch your body language** - nodding or smiling at certain answers encourages participants to give more of those answers.

::: tip Key takeaway
Usability studies are vulnerable to implicit bias, confirmation bias, the serial position effect, leading questions, friendliness bias, social desirability bias, and the Hawthorne effect. Reduce them by reflecting on your assumptions, recruiting diverse participants, setting criteria in advance, asking neutral questions, and keeping your reactions neutral.
:::
