# Accessibility in research

## Why accessibility belongs in the empathize phase

**Accessibility** means designing products, services, and environments so that people with disabilities can use them. You learned the basics in [Chapter 1](../foundations/04-user-centered-design), including [assistive technology](../foundations/05-assistive-technology). This page shows how to include accessibility from the very beginning, while you research and empathize.

People with disabilities have the same goals as everyone else. A resident who uses a wheelchair wants clean laundry just like everyone else in the building. What changes is the way they reach that goal. If you don't include their needs in your personas, user stories, and journey maps, your product will probably exclude them, and fixing that later is much harder.

It's impossible to guess every way a person might experience your product. That is why the best approach is to **include people with disabilities in your research** and learn directly from them.

## Permanent, temporary, and situational disabilities

Disability is not only a permanent condition. Many people experience limitations for a short time or in specific situations:

- **Permanent** - a long-term condition, for example a person who has one arm, is blind, or is deaf.
- **Temporary** - a short-term limitation, for example a broken wrist, an eye infection, or a lost voice.
- **Situational** - a limitation caused by the situation, for example holding a baby in one arm, reading a screen in bright sunlight, or being in a noisy room.

A person with one arm, a person with a broken wrist, and a parent holding a baby all need a product that works with one hand. Designing for the permanent case helps all three.

## Design considerations by type of ability

Here are some ideas to explore during research. They are only a starting point; real users will teach you much more.

### Touch

- **Reachable controls** - place important buttons where they are easy to reach with one hand and on different screen sizes.
- **Forgiving interactions** - protect important actions from accidental taps, for example with a confirmation or an undo option.
- **Keyboard support** - make sure everything works with a keyboard and with the one-handed keyboard modes that phones offer.
- **Customization** - let users rearrange or pin the features they use most often.

### Sight

- **Readable text** - use a comfortable font size and allow users to enlarge it.
- **Alternative text** - describe meaningful images and icons so screen readers can read them aloud.
- **Strong contrast** - choose color combinations with enough contrast between text and background.
- **More than color** - never use color alone to communicate meaning. A red field should also have an icon or a written error message.
- **Situational awareness** - consider users who can't look at the screen, for example while driving or walking.

### Hearing

- **More than sound** - don't rely only on sounds for alerts. Add visual signals and **haptics**, vibrations that the user can feel.
- **Captions** - provide captions or transcripts for all videos and audio content.
- **Written communication** - offer chat or messaging as an alternative to phone support.

### Speech

- **Written alternatives** - offer text-based options wherever a product expects voice input, such as voice assistants or automated phone menus.
- **Real-time text** - support services that let users type during a phone call.
- **Rich messaging** - allow emoji, images, and short replies in chat, which help people who find typing or speaking difficult.

### Learning and cognition

Neurodivergent users, for example people with dyslexia or ADHD, benefit from:

- **Simple designs** - short texts, clear structure, and few distractions.
- **Consistent navigation** - menus and buttons that stay in the same place across the whole product.
- **Familiar symbols** - widely recognized icons instead of creative but unclear ones.
- **Multiple formats** - text combined with images, video, or audio explanations.

### Customizable text

**Customizable text** lets users change how text looks: its size, font, color, line spacing, and letter spacing. It goes further than simple zooming and especially helps people with dyslexia or other visual processing difficulties, while the layout and functions keep working.

## Learn from assistive technology

To design for people who use [assistive technology](../foundations/05-assistive-technology), you need to understand how it works. Talk to people who use it, watch videos of experts using screen readers or switch devices, and try the accessibility features on your own phone and computer.

A good first step is **keyboard navigation**. Try to use a website without a mouse, only with the Tab, Enter, and arrow keys. Many people rely on the keyboard all the time, and a site that works well with a keyboard usually also works with other assistive technologies, such as switch devices.

## The curb-cut effect

A **curb cut** is the small ramp where a sidewalk meets the street. Curb cuts were introduced for wheelchair users, but today they help almost everyone: parents with strollers, travelers with suitcases, cyclists, delivery workers, and older people.

This is called the **curb-cut effect**: an improvement made for disabled people turns out to make life easier for many others too. Other examples are easy to find:

- **Captions** - designed for deaf and hard-of-hearing people, but also used in noisy bars, on quiet trains, and by people learning a language.
- **Audible and tactile crossing signals** - designed for blind people, but also helpful for distracted pedestrians looking at their phones.
- **Elevators and wide doors** - designed for wheelchair users, but also used by people with heavy luggage or a temporary injury.

### Example: session time limits

Many websites log users out after a few minutes of inactivity. That's a security feature, but it can make a form impossible to complete for someone who reads slowly, uses assistive technology, or is interrupted by a child. Allowing users to extend the time, warning them before it runs out, or removing the limit when it isn't needed helps all of these people. The international accessibility guidelines ([WCAG](https://www.w3.org/WAI/standards-guidelines/wcag/)) include this as a requirement.

### WashSlot example

WashSlot's visual and vibrating end-of-cycle alert was designed for Grace, who can't hear the machine's beep. It also helps Tomasz, who is often on work calls, and Kofi, who wears headphones while studying. That's the curb-cut effect in action.

::: tip Key takeaway
Include people with disabilities in your research from the start. Think about permanent, temporary, and situational disabilities, and consider touch, sight, hearing, speech, and cognition. Thanks to the curb-cut effect, accessible solutions usually make the product better for everyone.
:::
