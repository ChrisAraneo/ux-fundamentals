# Deceptive patterns

## What are deceptive patterns?

Not every misleading design is an accident caused by bias. Sometimes products are designed to mislead on purpose. **Deceptive patterns** are design techniques that push users into actions or purchases they never intended. They can be visual, interactive, written, audio, or motion elements, and they often appear in online shops, ads, sign-up flows, and apps.

The term was introduced in 2010 by the UX designer Harry Brignull, who originally called them "dark patterns". Many designers and companies now prefer "deceptive patterns", because the word describes the actual problem, deception, and avoids linking the word "dark" with something bad.

Deceptive patterns are **unethical**, and they're also **bad business**. When users realize they've been tricked, they lose trust in the brand, complain publicly, and leave. In many countries, some of these practices are also illegal under consumer protection laws.

## Common deceptive patterns

### Forced continuity

A free trial silently turns into a paid subscription, without any warning or reminder, and canceling is hard.

*Example:* you start a seven-day free trial of a language-learning app. On day eight, your card is charged, and the "Cancel" option is hidden deep in the settings.

**Do this instead:** remind users before the trial ends and before they're charged, make canceling as easy as signing up, link directly to the cancellation steps, and label every button clearly.

### Sneak into basket

Extra products are added to the user's cart automatically, so they have to notice them and remove them.

*Example:* when you book a bus ticket, travel insurance appears in the cart because a checkbox was already selected.

**Do this instead:** never preselect options that add items or costs. Users should only pay for what they chose.

### Hidden costs

Extra fees appear only at the very end of checkout, when users are tired and less likely to start over somewhere else.

*Example:* a concert ticket shows one price, but at the last step a "service fee" and a "processing fee" appear.

**Do this instead:** show the full price, including shipping, fees, and taxes, as early as possible, for example with a cost calculator before checkout.

### Confirmshaming

The option to say "no" is written to make users feel guilty or foolish.

*Example:* a newsletter pop-up offers two buttons: "Yes, sign me up" and "No thanks, I don't care about saving money."

**Do this instead:** use neutral, respectful wording for every option, such as "Subscribe" and "No thanks".

### Urgency

Artificial time pressure pushes users to decide before they've had time to think.

*Example:* a countdown timer says the "special price" ends in 9:59 minutes, but the timer restarts every time you reload the page.

**Do this instead:** show deadlines only when they're real, and give users enough time to make a calm decision.

### Scarcity

The product claims that only a few items are left, or that many people are looking at it, to trigger fear of missing out. These numbers are often fake.

*Example:* a hotel website says "Only 1 room left! 14 people are looking at this hotel."

**Do this instead:** show stock information only when it's true and useful. Aim to inform, not to pressure.

These are only some of the deceptive patterns that exist. Others include, for example, making it easy to subscribe but very hard to unsubscribe, or disguising ads as normal content.

## Why deceptive patterns work

Deceptive patterns are harder to resist online than in a real shop. Imagine a salesperson who follows you around a store, interrupts you every ten seconds with "only four left in your size!", brings you products you didn't ask for, and pushes you to sign up for a store card. You would probably leave annoyed. Online, the same tactics appear as small banners, pop-ups, and preselected checkboxes, and many people don't notice them.

Notifications are another example. A red badge on an app icon creates a feeling of urgency. Each new notification gives the brain a small dose of **dopamine**, a chemical released when something pleasant happens, especially when it comes with a sound or movement. When notifications stop, some people feel anxious and keep checking their phones.

## The attention economy

Every product competes for people's limited time and attention. This competition is called the **attention economy**. The idea is often linked to the economist and psychologist Herbert A. Simon, who wrote that "a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention."

People are not good at multitasking, and constant distractions make it harder to finish tasks well. Products designed to capture as much attention as possible can encourage addictive behavior and contribute to stress, anxiety, or fear of missing out. Even small details, like bright colors and loud notification sounds, add to the problem.

Some companies have started to respond, for example with screen-time dashboards, focus modes, and phones that silence notifications while you drive. These features show that design can respect people's time.

As a designer, you can help:

- **Avoid deceptive patterns** - be honest with users, and think about whether a design matches your own values.
- **Understand business goals and metrics** - notice when a business goal, such as "time spent in the app", conflicts with what's good for users.
- **Share good practices** - you have influence in your team. Use it to steer decisions in the right direction.
- **Speak up** - if you notice deceptive patterns in a product you work on, raise the issue with your team early. Fighting them is a team effort.

Remember: good UX serves users **and** the business at the same time. There are many honest ways to attract and keep users.

::: tip Key takeaway
Deceptive patterns trick users into actions they didn't intend, such as forced continuity, sneak into basket, hidden costs, confirmshaming, urgency, and scarcity. They are unethical and damage trust. Design honestly, respect users' attention, and speak up when you see deceptive patterns.
:::
