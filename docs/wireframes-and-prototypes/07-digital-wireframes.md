# Digital wireframes

## When are you ready to go digital?

Paper is great for exploring, but at some point you need cleaner, more precise wireframes. Before you switch to a design tool, ask yourself three questions:

1. **Is my paper wireframe complete?** - "Complete" doesn't mean finished; it means you know the structure you want.
2. **Have I received feedback?** - ideally, teammates or a mentor have already commented on your sketches.
3. **Am I ready for basic visual decisions?** - for example, using different text sizes to show what is a heading and what is body text.

If the answer to all three is "yes", you're ready. If not, keep iterating on paper.

## What changes in digital wireframes

### Use real words where they matter

Write real text for elements that explain function, such as section headings ("Free machines now") and labels under navigation icons ("My bookings"). This makes the purpose of each element clear.

### Use placeholder text for long content

For longer blocks of text, such as descriptions or house rules, use **placeholder text**. The most common is **Lorem ipsum**, scrambled Latin text based on a work by the Roman philosopher Cicero. Printers have used it for centuries to show where text will go without distracting readers with real meaning, and designers still use it today.

### Don't add color or images yet

Keep the wireframe in grayscale and focus on structure and function. Expressive details, such as brand colors, photos, and illustrations, come later in high-fidelity designs.

## Why create digital wireframes?

Digital wireframes have two main advantages:

- **More precision** - paper sketches can be messy; digital wireframes let you get the structure, alignment, and sizes right.
- **Easier sharing** - instead of photographing or photocopying sketches, you send a link. Teammates can comment directly in the file and even work on it at the same time.

## Design tools

There are many tools for creating wireframes and prototypes, such as **Figma**, **Penpot**, and **Sketch**. This chapter uses Figma, a popular tool that runs in the browser and combines design, prototyping, and collaboration. Figma offers a free plan, and students and teachers can usually get extra features for free by verifying their education status.

Don't get too attached to one tool. Tools change, new ones appear, and employers use different ones. Most of them work in a similar way, so once you know one, you can learn another quickly. A good way to learn any tool is to simply play with it: draw a circle, change its color, add a border, type some text, and change the font. When you get stuck, use the tool's help center, video tutorials, or ask colleagues. Remember that a tool is only a means to an end, not the goal itself.

## Figma basics

Figma's interface changes from time to time, so menus may look slightly different from what you see here, but the main concepts stay the same:

- **Canvas** - the large, almost endless workspace where you place all your designs.
- **Frames** - containers for your design. Each frame usually represents one screen. Figma offers preset frame sizes for popular phones, tablets, and desktop screens.
- **Layers panel** - the list on the left side of all frames, shapes, and text in the file. Layers inside a frame are nested under it.
- **Toolbar** - the tools for creating frames, shapes, text, and more.
- **Properties panel** - the panel on the right where you change the position, size, fill, border (stroke), and other properties of the selected element.

### Useful tricks

- **Hold Shift** while drawing to create a perfectly straight line, a perfect circle, or a square.
- **Copy and paste** with Ctrl+C and Ctrl+V (Cmd+C and Cmd+V on a Mac) to repeat elements quickly.
- **Use the red guides** that appear while you move elements; they help you align elements with each other and with the frame.
- **Pan and zoom** by holding the space bar and dragging, and by pressing Ctrl or Cmd with plus or minus.
- **Type numbers** into the size and position fields for precise values.

## From paper to digital: an example

Here is how the WashSlot designer recreates the paper sketch of the home screen in Figma:

1. **Bring in the sketch** - drag a photo of the paper wireframe onto the canvas as a reference.
2. **Create a frame** - choose a phone preset and place it next to the sketch.
3. **Draw the top bar** - draw a rectangle across the top of the frame.
4. **Add the navigation icon** - draw one short horizontal line while holding Shift, then copy and paste it twice and align the three lines with the guides.
5. **Add the title and avatar** - type "WashSlot" with the text tool and draw a circle (with Shift) for the avatar, with a smaller circle and a few lines for a simple person icon.
6. **Create the machine list** - draw one row with a circle crossed by an X, a "Machine 1" label, and a line for the status. Then copy the row three times and add dividers between the rows.
7. **Add the main button** - draw a rectangle labeled "Book a slot".
8. **Add the bottom navigation** - draw a bar with three small squares and the labels "Machines", "Report", and "My bookings".
9. **Use grays only** - use light and dark grays to separate areas, and keep color for later.
10. **Name the frame** - rename it in the layers panel, for example "1 Home", so it's easy to find later.

Then repeat these steps for the other screens of the user flow.

::: tip Key takeaway
Move to digital wireframes when your paper structure is clear and has received feedback. Use real words for important labels, Lorem ipsum for long text, and grays instead of color. Digital wireframes are more precise and easier to share, and tools like Figma make them quick to build.
:::
