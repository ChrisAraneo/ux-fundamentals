# Gestalt principles

## What are Gestalt principles?

**Gestalt principles** explain how people instinctively group what they see, spot patterns, and turn complex scenes into simple shapes. "Gestalt" is a German word meaning "shape" or "form". The principles come from a school of psychology founded in the early 20th century.

The core idea is that people see **the whole before the parts**. When you look at clouds and spot a shape that looks like a rabbit, your brain is connecting separate elements into a meaningful whole. In design, you can use these natural tendencies to make interfaces easier to understand at a glance.

The look of a design may feel subjective, but how people perceive it follows predictable patterns. Gestalt principles help you use those patterns on purpose.

There are many Gestalt principles. Three of them are especially useful for wireframes: **similarity**, **proximity**, and **common region**.

## Similarity

**Similarity** means that items sharing a shape, size, or color appear to do the same thing.

A familiar example is links on a web page: they usually share the same color and underline, so people recognize every link as something clickable.

*WashSlot example:* all four machines on the home screen are drawn as rows of the same size, with the same icon shape and status label. Users immediately understand that each row works the same way: tapping it shows the details of that machine.

**How to use it:** give a shared look to elements that do the same job, for example all user avatars the same shape and size. And make elements with different functions look different, so users don't confuse them.

## Proximity

**Proximity** means that elements placed close together are seen as more related than elements that are far apart.

*WashSlot example:* on the summary screen, the reminder switch sits directly under the booking time it belongs to. If it were at the bottom of the screen, far from the time, users might not understand which booking it affects.

**How to use it:** put related elements close together, such as a label and its input field, or a "Read more" link and the text it expands. Leave more space between groups that are not related. A few seconds of confusion can be enough for a user to give up.

## Common region

**Common region** means that elements inside the same closed area, for example within a border or on the same background, are seen as a group.

*WashSlot example:* each machine row is placed inside its own card with a light border. The machine name, status, and remaining time all appear in the same card, so users see them as information about one machine.

Another everyday example is the navigation bar at the bottom of many apps. A different background color or a border around it makes its icons read as one group of main sections, instead of icons floating on the screen.

**How to use it:** use borders, cards, and background blocks to group related elements.

## Other Gestalt principles

Once you are comfortable with these three, explore others, such as:

- **Figure and ground** - people separate an object (the figure) from its background, which is why pop-up windows often darken the page behind them.
- **Continuity** - the eye follows lines and curves, so elements arranged in a line or curve seem related.
- **Closure** - people complete incomplete shapes in their minds, so a circle with a small gap still looks like a circle.

::: tip Key takeaway
Gestalt principles describe how people see groups and patterns. Use similarity to give a shared look to elements that do the same job, proximity to place related elements close together, and common region to group elements inside a shared area. Your wireframes will become easier to understand.
:::
