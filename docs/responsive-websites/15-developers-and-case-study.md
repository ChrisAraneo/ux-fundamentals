# Developers and the case study

## Who builds a website?

When your design is finished, developers (also called engineers) turn it into working code. You hand over the mockups, the prototype, the specifications, and your annotations, and the coding begins. You learned the general process in [Handoff and case study](../high-fidelity-design/17-handoff-and-case-study). Website teams usually include two kinds of developers:

- **Front-end developers** - write the code for everything users see and interact with in the browser, based on the designer's specifications. They're often simply called web developers. Layouts, breakpoints, hover and focus states, and animations are their territory.
- **Back-end developers** - write the code that runs behind the scenes: the website's architecture and the way data is stored and retrieved, based on the sitemap and the required functionality. Users rarely see their work, but they feel it: a page that loads quickly is often the result of good back-end engineering.

You'll work with both. Front-end developers will ask about spacing, states, and what happens at each breakpoint. Back-end developers will ask about data, for example what a search should return or what happens when two people save something at the same moment.

### Is the design ready?

Before you hand anything over, ask:

1. **Does the design show the intended experience?** - on every screen size you planned.
2. **Is the content final?** - placeholder text, icons, and images have been replaced with final assets.
3. **Can people use it without help?** - test participants understand it and complete tasks without guidance.

When the answer to all three is yes, the design is ready for development. Each team works in its own way, so talk to the developers early, agree on how you'll collaborate, and stay available for questions. Good communication matters more than any particular process.

*WashSlot example:* the same two freelance developers who built the app build the website. The front-end developer asks how the six machine cards behave between the tablet and desktop breakpoints, so the designer adds notes for the in-between widths. The back-end developer reuses the app's booking data, so a slot booked on the website instantly disappears from the app, and the other way around.

## Adding the website to your portfolio

Your portfolio now gets its second project. As you know from [Chapter 2](../design-process/01-ux-portfolio), **case studies** are the heart of a portfolio. A case study is a short, visual presentation of a project that covers its goal, your role, the process you followed, and the outcome. Recruiters and hiring managers read case studies to see your design knowledge, how you work with others, and whether you can take an idea from start to finish despite obstacles. Keep it concise, visual, and easy to skim.

A responsive website lets you show something your app case study couldn't: that you can design across screen sizes and devices. Make the most of it by showing the same page on desktop, tablet, and phone.

### Sections of a website case study

1. **Overview** - a short description of the product, the project's length, the problem, and the goals.
2. **Your role** - what you were responsible for, and who else was involved if you worked in a team.
3. **Understanding the users** - your research, personas, problem statements, journey maps, and sitemap. If you reused research from the app project, say so and explain what you added.
4. **Starting the design** - paper and digital wireframes, plotted layouts for different screen sizes, and the low-fidelity prototype, with notes on why you made key decisions.
5. **Usability study** - a short, plain-language description of your method and your most important findings, and how you reached them.
6. **Refining the design** - before-and-after examples of mockups you changed based on the study, followed by the key final mockups in all screen sizes.
7. **Prototype** - a link to the working high-fidelity prototype and a short description of the main flow. Check that the link works for people outside your team.
8. **Accessibility** - examples of how the design supports people with disabilities, such as focus order, labels, contrast, and zoom.
9. **Takeaways and next steps** - the impact of your work, what you learned, and what you'd do next if the project continued.
10. **Contact** - make it easy for readers to reach you.

Many designers build the case study as a slide deck first, which is easy to share in job applications, and later publish it on their portfolio website. Keep your earliest sketches: they show your process, and employers want to see how you think, not just the final screens.

*WashSlot example:* the website case study opens with one image: the homepage on a laptop, the laundry room tablet, and a phone, side by side. It explains why a website was needed alongside the app, shows the website sitemap and the plotted layouts for three screen sizes, and includes two before-and-after pairs: the carousel arrows that failed the contrast check, and the "Report" link that the critique added to broken machine cards. The accessibility section shows the focus order annotations and the 400% zoom view. The next steps are an automatic logout on the shared tablet and a version of the website in the two most common languages among residents after the local one.

::: tip Key takeaway
Front-end developers build what users see in the browser; back-end developers build the architecture and data behind it. Hand off when the design shows the intended experience, uses final content, and works without help. Then add the website as a second case study to your portfolio, and use it to show that you can design for many screen sizes.
:::
