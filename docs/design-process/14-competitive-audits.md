# Competitive audits

## What is a competitive audit?

A **competitive audit** is a structured review of your competitors' strengths and weaknesses. It helps you learn from products that already exist: what works, what doesn't, and what nobody offers yet. It's one of many tools you can use during ideation.

A competitive audit helps you:

- **Identify key competitors** - know who else is solving a similar problem.
- **Review their products** - see what they offer and how their products work.
- **Understand their positioning** - learn how they present themselves and whom they target.
- **Judge their quality** - find out where they shine and where they fall short.

## Direct and indirect competitors

There are two types of competitors, and a good audit includes both:

- **Direct competitors** - offer a similar product to the same audience. You are both trying to solve the same problem for the same people.
- **Indirect competitors** - either offer a similar product to a different audience, or a different product to the same audience.

*WashSlot example:*

- **Direct** - other apps for booking machines in shared laundry rooms.
- **Indirect** - apps that show live machine status in commercial laundromats (similar product, different audience), and general shared-calendar apps that some buildings use for booking (different product, same audience).

## Why competitive audits matter

Competitive audits are useful for several reasons:

- **They inform your design** - seeing how others approached the problem helps you make better decisions.
- **They reveal usability problems to avoid** - if a competitor's booking flow is confusing, you know what not to do.
- **They show gaps in the market** - needs that no competitor meets are opportunities for your product.
- **They provide evidence** - they give you facts to support your design decisions when talking to stakeholders.
- **They save time and money** - you learn from others' successes and mistakes instead of repeating them.

## Limitations

Competitive audits are helpful, but they have limits:

- **They can limit creativity** - focusing too much on competitors can lead to copying instead of innovating. Use the audit as a starting point, not a blueprint.
- **They depend on interpretation** - the results are only as good as your analysis. Discussing findings with teammates helps.
- **Not every solution fits your users** - a feature that works for a competitor's audience may be useless for yours.
- **They get outdated** - markets change, and new competitors appear. Repeat audits regularly.

## How to conduct a competitive audit

1. **Set specific goals** - decide what exactly you want to compare. "Compare the user experience" is too broad. "Compare how new and returning users book a machine" is much more useful.
2. **List your competitors** - choose a manageable number, usually between three and ten, with a mix of direct and indirect competitors. Ask the client or team whom they see as competitors, and search online for similar products.
3. **Choose what to compare** - list the aspects you will evaluate. Put competitors in the rows of a spreadsheet and aspects in the columns.
4. **Research each competitor** - use each product as your users would. Write down the strong and weak points, and judge whether each product fits your users. Take plenty of screenshots.
5. **Analyze the findings** - look for trends and patterns. What do most competitors do the same way? Where do they all fail?
6. **Summarize in a report** - present the most important findings and what they mean for your product.

### What to compare

Besides general information, such as location, product offering, price, business size, target audience, and unique value proposition, audits usually evaluate four areas:

- **First impressions** - how does the product feel at first glance, on desktop and on mobile? Is it responsive? Does the design fit the product?
- **Interaction** - is navigation clear? Is it obvious what can be clicked? Is the product accessible, for example with a screen reader or in several languages?
- **Visual design** - is the branding consistent across pages? Does it suit the audience? Are the colors and images memorable?
- **Content** - is the text clear and easy to scan? Does the tone of voice match the brand? Can users find the information they need?

### Use a rating scale

A consistent rating scale makes competitors easier to compare. For example:

1. **Needs work** - this aspect doesn't work, and users don't get what they need.
2. **Okay** - this aspect has problems, but it could meet user needs with some work.
3. **Good** - this aspect works well, but not every piece of information is easy to find.
4. **Outstanding** - this aspect is consistent and meets or exceeds user needs.

## Writing the audit report

The report turns your spreadsheet into insights that others can understand quickly. A useful report answers these questions:

1. **What were the goals of the audit?**
2. **Who are the key competitors?** - one or two sentences each, including whether they are direct or indirect.
3. **What do they offer, and how good is it?** - a short paragraph per competitor.
4. **How do they position themselves?** - their target audience and the kind of customers they want.
5. **How do they describe themselves?** - their own value proposition.
6. **What are their strengths?** - two to four things each competitor does well.
7. **What are their weaknesses?** - two to four things each competitor could do better.
8. **What gaps exist?** - needs that no competitor meets.
9. **What are your opportunities?** - two or three ways your product can stand out.

*WashSlot example:* the audit shows that no competitor supports residents without smartphones and that all of them are available in only one language. Under **Opportunities**, the designer writes: "Offer a shared tablet in the laundry room and translated house rules."

## Presenting the audit

You can share an audit as a written report or as a slide presentation. Reports are faster to create and easy to read when people have little time. Presentations are more engaging but take more preparation.

### How to structure your presentation

Use a simple structure:

1. **Introduction** - goals, research questions, and the method you used.
2. **Sections** - one section per topic, each starting with a clear title slide.
3. **Findings** - what you learned about competitors and how your product compares, including where it is stronger and where it needs work.
4. **Summary** - the key takeaways, because an audience can't remember details about ten competitors.

### Tips for presenting

- **Choose the right format for data** - use charts for numbers, screenshots or short screen recordings for specific problems, and explain your rating scale.
- **Limit text on slides** - keep slides simple and save the details for what you say or for an appendix.
- **Get feedback first** - show your slides to a teammate before the real presentation.
- **Practice** - rehearse a few times and use notes to stay on topic and on time.
- **Stay objective** - be aware of your own biases and support every conclusion with evidence.

## From audit to ideas

Finally, use the audit to generate more ideas. Bring together people with different perspectives and ask questions such as: "Two competitors have a complicated sign-up process. How could ours be easier?" Group similar ideas to spot patterns, and aim to leave the session with a list of ways your product can stand out.

::: tip Key takeaway
A competitive audit compares direct and indirect competitors against specific goals and aspects, such as first impressions, interaction, visual design, and content. Use it to find gaps and opportunities, not to copy, and repeat it regularly.
:::
