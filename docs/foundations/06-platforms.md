# Designing for different platforms

## What is a platform?

A **platform** is the medium on which people use your product: a desktop computer, a website on a phone, a mobile app, a tablet, a smartwatch, a TV, a smart display, a car dashboard, and more.

In the past, many designers only had to think about websites on desktop computers. Today, the same person might check a service on their phone in the morning, on a laptop at work, and on a TV in the evening. **Users expect a product to look and feel familiar on every platform**, so designers have to plan for many devices and screen sizes.

## Start with one platform

Even though you need to think about many platforms, **start a new product on a single platform**. Choose the one that best fits your users' needs. For example, a food delivery service is mostly used on the go, so mobile is a natural starting point. A tool for editing long documents is probably used mostly on laptops. Once the first platform works well, you can design for the others.

Across all platforms, also keep a **consistent brand identity**: the same visual style, tone of voice, and recognizable elements, so users always know they are using the same product.

## Mobile vs. desktop

People use phones and computers differently:

- **Mobile sessions are usually short** - people check something quickly while waiting for a bus or standing in a queue. Desktop sessions tend to be longer and more focused.
- **Mobile use keeps growing** - in many parts of the world, a phone is the main or only way people go online.
- **Mobile connections can be limited** - slower networks, older devices, and expensive data plans mean that pages must load fast and stay light.

## Golden rules of mobile design

1. **Put the main action front and center** - the most important call-to-action button (for example, "Order" or "Book") should be easy to see and easy to reach with a thumb.
2. **Keep navigation short and simple** - show only the core functions in the menu and move everything else to secondary screens.
3. **Use familiar gestures** - rely on gestures people already know, like tapping, swiping, and scrolling, instead of inventing new ones.
4. **Support both orientations** - consider how the design works when the phone is held vertically (portrait) and horizontally (landscape).
5. **Reduce visual clutter** - small screens fill up quickly, so remove anything that doesn't help the user complete their task.

## Things to consider when designing across platforms

### Screen size

Desktop and laptop screens give you plenty of space. On small screens, you have to **decide what matters most** and show that first. A good exercise is to design the mobile version first: if something doesn't fit on a phone, ask whether it is needed at all.

### Interaction

People interact with each platform differently: a mouse and keyboard on a computer, fingers on a phone, a remote control on a TV, voice on a smart speaker. Each way of interacting affects your design decisions. Also remember that people may use assistive technology, such as screen readers, captions, or switch devices, on every platform.

### Content layout

**Layout** is the way information is arranged on the screen:

- **Desktop** - usually wide (landscape) screens, where content can be arranged in several columns.
- **Mobile phone** - usually tall (portrait) screens, which work well for scrolling content in a single column. Supporting landscape mode as well takes more design work but gives users more flexibility.
- **Tablet** - a mix of both, so you can borrow layout ideas from desktop and mobile.
- **Smartwatch** - a very small round or square screen with room for only the most essential information.

A common technique for websites is **responsive web design**: the layout adapts automatically to the size of the screen, for example by turning three columns on desktop into one column on a phone.

### Functionality

People often choose a platform based on **what they want to do**. They might browse products on a phone during a commute, but finish a large purchase on a laptop at home. Think about how and when users will need your product on each platform, and adjust the features accordingly.

::: tip Key takeaway
Users switch between many devices and expect a consistent experience. Start with the platform that suits your users best, then adapt screen size, interaction, layout, and features for each additional platform.
:::
