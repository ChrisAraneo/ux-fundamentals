# Bias in design

## Bias doesn't stop at research

In [Chapter 1](../foundations/13-bias-in-research), you learned how biases can distort UX research. They can also slip into the designs themselves. Because many biases are unconscious, designers can create products that exclude, belittle, or mislead people without ever meaning to.

A **bias** is an unfair tendency to judge something or someone positively or negatively, without enough information. Other words for it are prejudice, preconception, or partiality.

## Implicit bias in design

**Implicit bias** is the set of attitudes and stereotypes we connect with groups of people without realizing it. These attitudes can feel like truths, even when they're wrong.

Here's an everyday example. A shop manager has two candidates for a job: a 20-year-old without experience and a 55-year-old with decades of experience in retail. If the manager unconsciously believes that older people learn new systems slowly, they might reject the experienced candidate without even looking at their skills. Age alone tells nothing about a person's abilities, but the bias decides anyway.

The same thing happens in product design:

- **Assumptions about names** - a delivery app's sign-up form requires a first name and a last name and rejects names with hyphens or apostrophes. People whose names don't follow the pattern the designer had in mind can't create an account.
- **Assumptions about families** - a school app has fields only for "mother" and "father". Children with one parent, two fathers, or a grandparent as their guardian don't fit, and their families feel excluded.
- **Assumptions about language** - the first version of WashSlot showed the house rules only in the local language, because the designer assumed that "all residents speak it". Kofi, and others like him, would have been left out.

These designs are not only unfair. They're also bad for business, because they push away users.

### Personas help, but only if they're inclusive

[Personas](../design-process/06-personas) and [user journey maps](../design-process/08-user-journey-maps) are some of the best tools against implicit bias, but only if you create them carefully. Don't assume that people of a particular ethnicity, gender, age, ability, or income are, or aren't, part of your audience. Users are complex people with many different needs, and research, not assumptions, should tell you who they are.

## Cognitive biases in design

A **cognitive bias** is a systematic error in thinking: a predictable way in which context and the presentation of information influence our judgment and decisions. Here are some biases that commonly affect UX work:

- **Ambiguity effect** - preferring options with a known outcome over options with an uncertain one, even when the uncertain option might be better.
- **Clustering illusion** - seeing patterns or trends in random data where none exist.
- **Confirmation bias** - interpreting information in a way that supports what you already believe.
- **Cultural bias** - judging words or behavior by the norms of your own culture instead of the culture of the person you're studying.
- **False consensus bias** - assuming that your own opinions and habits are common and that different ones are unusual.
- **Framing effect** - letting the wording or presentation of information, not the facts, drive a decision.
- **Fundamental attribution error** - explaining someone's behavior by their personality while ignoring the situation they're in.
- **Hindsight bias** - believing, after something happens, that it was easy to predict.
- **Implicit bias** - unconscious attitudes and stereotypes about groups of people.
- **Racial bias** - believing that people of a certain racial group share particular traits, which often leads to discrimination.
- **Serial position effect** - recalling the beginning and the end of a list more easily than its middle.
- **Social desirability bias** - answering in a way that makes you look good to others.
- **Sunk cost fallacy** - continuing with something because you've already invested in it, even when the costs now outweigh the benefits.
- **Survivorship bias** - focusing on successful examples and ignoring the ones that failed.
- **Transparency illusion** - overestimating how well others understand your thoughts, or how well you understand theirs.

## When bias reaches real products

Bias in design and non-inclusive research have caused well-known product failures:

- **Car safety** - for decades, crash test dummies were modeled mainly on an average male body, so safety features were tested less well for women and smaller people.
- **Automatic soap dispensers** - some sensors failed to detect darker skin, because they were tested mostly on lighter skin.
- **Airport body scanners** - some scanners flag thick or textured hair, head coverings, and transgender travelers' bodies as suspicious more often, which leads to extra, embarrassing searches.
- **"Real name" policies** - platforms that required legal names caused problems for transgender people, people with names in other writing systems, and people who need to protect their identity.

In each case, the people who were harmed were not considered, or not represented, when the product was designed and tested.

## How to limit bias in your work

1. **Learn about biases** - you can only notice biases if you know they exist.
2. **Build self-awareness** - examine your own assumptions, try to see situations from other people's perspectives, and question your first instincts.
3. **Notice bias in others** - recognize bias in teammates and stakeholders, and speak up respectfully when you see it.
4. **Treat ideas as hypotheses** - every design idea is a guess. Be ready to change direction when research shows you were wrong.
5. **Run a pre-mortem** - at the start of a project, imagine that it has failed, and brainstorm what could have caused it. This helps you spot overlooked groups and risks early.
6. **Work with diverse people** - diverse teams and diverse test participants catch biases that a homogeneous group would miss. It's much easier to treat someone as an "edge case" when nobody like them is in the room.

::: tip Key takeaway
Everyone has biases, and they can slip into designs as easily as into research. Learn the common types, question your assumptions, build inclusive personas, treat every idea as a hypothesis, and include diverse people in your team and your tests.
:::
