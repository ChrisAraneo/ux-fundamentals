# Common screens and navigation

## Common screens in mobile apps

Apps solve very different problems, but most of them share the same types of screens. Each type has a clear purpose and well-known patterns. Knowing these patterns gives you a head start and helps users, because they already know how these screens usually work.

### Home screen

The **home screen** is where people land after opening an app. It shows the main content and the most important actions, and it's the starting point for everything else. Because it tells users what the app offers, it needs to be easy to understand. Familiar elements, such as a bottom navigation bar and a search icon, help people get started. Resist the temptation to show everything here: focus on what's essential, and use negative space.

### Onboarding screens

**Onboarding** screens introduce new users to an app's main features, especially features that might be unfamiliar. They can also ask a few questions to personalize the experience. A good onboarding flow is short, uses clear words and simple graphics, and always lets people skip ahead, because many users want to start right away.

### Profile or account screen

A **profile** (or **account**) screen holds a user's personal details, such as their name and email address, and their preferences, such as notification and accessibility settings. Because it holds many important details, keep it simple, clearly grouped, and easy to scan.

### Checkout screen

A **checkout** screen is where users pay for something. Checkout often includes several forms, and it can feel stressful, because money and personal data are involved. Help users feel safe: explain what each field needs, confirm when a field is filled in correctly, show that their data is protected, and display a clear confirmation when the payment succeeds.

*WashSlot example:* WashSlot has no payment, because laundry costs are part of the building's service charge. Its "checkout" is the **summary screen**, where residents review the machine, day, and time before confirming. Onboarding is just three short screens: what WashSlot does, how to book, and how reminders work, with a "Skip" link on each.

## Types of navigation

**Navigation** is the set of elements that lets people move through a product. Simple, predictable navigation is one of the most important parts of a good experience. Here are four common types.

### Navigation bar

A **navigation bar** shows links to the most important sections in one bar, usually along the top or bottom of the screen (on mobile, a **bottom navigation bar** is very common). Users can see all main sections at once and reach each one with a single tap. It works best for three to five sections.

### Tab bar

A **tab bar** shows a row of tabs that switch between views of related content, usually within one section, without leaving the screen. Tabs can show text, icons, or both, and the active tab is highlighted, for example with an underline or a different color.

### Navigation menu

A **navigation menu** opens when users tap an icon of three horizontal lines, often called the **hamburger menu** because of its shape. The menu is hidden until needed, which keeps the screen clean and leaves room for many links. The downside is that hidden options are easy to overlook, so don't hide the most important destinations there.

### Navigation hub

A **navigation hub** shows all main links on the home screen. Users go to a section and then return to the hub to go somewhere else. A hub keeps each screen simple and works well for apps with few sections, but it's slow for tasks that require jumping between sections.

*WashSlot example:* WashSlot combines three types. A **bottom navigation bar** links to the three main sections: "Machines" (the home screen), "Report", and "My bookings". Less frequent destinations, such as "Settings" and "House rules", sit in the **navigation menu** behind the menu icon in the top bar. On the booking screen, a **tab bar** with the next seven days lets residents switch between days without leaving the screen.

## Affordances

Navigation is built from elements like buttons, links, and icons. For people to use them, they must look usable. An **affordance** is a visual cue that shows what an element does and how to use it. A raised button suggests "press me", an underlined word suggests "tap to open", and a small arrow next to a field suggests "tap to see more options".

If an element has no affordance, users may never discover it. For example, plain text without any styling doesn't look tappable. Add a cue, such as a color, an underline, an icon, or a button shape, so people know they can interact with it.

*WashSlot example:* the day tabs on the booking screen show four days at a time. To show that more days are available, the last visible tab is partly cut off at the right edge of the screen. This small detail is an affordance: it suggests that the row continues and can be scrolled.

::: tip Key takeaway
Most apps share common screens: home, onboarding, profile, and checkout. Use their familiar patterns as a starting point. Choose navigation that fits your content: a navigation bar, tab bar, navigation menu, navigation hub, or a combination. Give every interactive element an affordance, a visual cue that shows people they can use it.
:::
