# Fidelity and wireframes

## What is a design?

In this course, a **design** is a solution to a real problem that users have. A strong design always keeps the user at its center, at every level of detail.

## Fidelity

**Fidelity** describes how closely a design resembles the finished product in appearance and behavior. You can imagine it as a dial that you turn up or down:

- **Low fidelity (lo-fi)** - simple, rough, and unpolished. Use it when you want to sketch many ideas fast while keeping your options open.
- **High fidelity (hi-fi)** - detailed and polished, close to the real product. Use it when you want users to react to something that feels real and give you specific feedback.

Think about planning a garden. A quick pencil sketch with circles for trees and rectangles for flower beds is low fidelity. A detailed plan with plant names, colors, and paths drawn to scale is high fidelity. Both are useful at different moments.

This chapter focuses on low-fidelity designs. You will work with high fidelity in a later chapter.

## What is a wireframe?

A **wireframe** is a simple skeleton of a digital screen, such as an app view or a web page. It is made mostly of lines, simple shapes, and a little text, which makes it look as if it were built from wire.

### Why create wireframes?

Wireframes have three main purposes:

1. **They set the basic structure** - they show how a page is organized before colors, images, and fonts are added. Ask yourself: "How can I arrange this information so it makes sense to users?"
2. **They show the intended function** - each element's purpose should be clear from the way it's drawn. A button should look like something you can press.
3. **They save time and money** - trying out different layouts with wireframes is fast, and the agreed wireframe guides the whole team later.

### Benefits of wireframing

Some teams want to skip wireframes to save time, but that usually costs more time later. Wireframes:

- **Show which elements belong on a page** - seeing them laid out helps the team decide whether anything is missing or unnecessary.
- **Catch problems early** - you can spot missing, misplaced, or disorganized elements and confusing navigation between pages.
- **Keep stakeholders focused on structure** - with no colors or images to discuss, conversations stay on layout and function.
- **Reduce rework** - when engineers and stakeholders agree on wireframes early, fewer changes are needed later.
- **Make iteration easy** - you can explore many design options quickly.

## How to draw a wireframe

You don't need to be an artist. Drawing is a skill that improves with practice, and in UX, a good wireframe is about **communicating information clearly**, not about beauty. Some designers draw loose, messy sketches; others use rulers. Both are fine.

### Elements

**Elements** are the building blocks of a design. Almost every wireframe is made from just three of them: lines, shapes (rectangles and circles), and text.

### Industry standards

To make wireframes easy for anyone to read, designers follow a few common conventions:

- **Text** - draw paragraphs as horizontal lines, and write out only short labels and headings.
- **Images** - draw any picture, photo, illustration, or icon as a box (or circle) crossed with an X.
- **Calls to action** - buttons are drawn as rectangles or circles, whichever matches the shape of the button.

### Common interface parts

These parts appear in many app screens and are useful to know by name:

- **Top bar** - a bar across the top of the screen with the screen title and actions related to it.
- **Navigation menu icon** - three horizontal lines (often called a "hamburger" icon) that open the main menu.
- **Avatar** - a circle with a simple person icon that represents the user's profile.
- **Divider** - a thin line that separates items in a list or groups content.
- **Carousel** - a set of images or cards that users can swipe through, one at a time.
- **Pagination indicator** - small dots that show how many items a carousel has and which one is visible.
- **Floating action button** - a round button, usually in the bottom corner, for the screen's main action.
- **Bottom navigation bar** - a bar at the bottom with icons for the app's main sections.

### Example: the WashSlot home screen

Here is how you might draw the WashSlot home screen, step by step:

1. **Frame** - draw a tall rectangle for the phone screen, big enough to draw inside.
2. **Top bar** - draw a bar across the top. Add a navigation menu icon on the left, the title "WashSlot" in the middle, and an avatar on the right.
3. **Machine list** - below the top bar, draw four rows separated by dividers. In each row, draw a circle with an X (the machine icon) on the left, the label "Machine 1" to "Machine 4", and a short horizontal line for the status text.
4. **Main action** - near the bottom, draw a wide rectangle labeled "Book a slot".
5. **Bottom navigation** - draw a bar at the bottom with three small squares for icons, and short labels under them: "Machines", "Report", and "My bookings".

That's it: lines, shapes, and a few words.

## Practice: wireframe an existing app

A great way to learn is to work backwards. Open an app you use often, such as a weather or music app, and draw a wireframe of its main screen:

1. **Draw the frame** - its size should roughly match the screen's proportions, so elements fit in similar places.
2. **Add the key elements** - use lines and simple shapes and follow the industry standards above.
3. **Review the whole drawing** - step back and check whether all key elements fit in the frame, whether anything important is missing, and whether text, images, and buttons are drawn according to the standards.

This final review of the whole drawing is important. On the job, you will also review other designers' wireframes in the same way.

::: tip Key takeaway
Fidelity describes how close a design is to the final product. Wireframes are low-fidelity outlines made of lines, shapes, and text. They set the structure, show the function of each element, and save time. Follow simple standards: lines for text, boxes with an X for images, and rectangles or circles for buttons.
:::
