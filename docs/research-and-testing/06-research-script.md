# The research script

## What is a script?

The **script**, also called a **discussion guide**, is the last element of a research plan. It lists everything you'll say during a session: the introduction, the tasks participants will try, and the follow-up questions you'll ask.

Reading from a script can feel a bit robotic, but it has two important advantages:

- **You don't forget anything** - every instruction and question is written down, so you don't realize afterward that you skipped the most important question.
- **Every participant hears the same thing** - consistent wording makes the sessions comparable.

## Tips for writing questions

1. **Use the same questions for everyone** - each session should cover the same features in the same way. Don't improvise different questions for different people.
2. **Ask open-ended questions** - questions like "How did you feel about...?" or "What bothered you about...?" invite stories. Avoid questions that can be answered with "yes" or "no".
3. **Encourage elaboration** - when someone gives a short answer, such as "I didn't like that screen", follow up with "Tell me more about that."
4. **Ask about the same topic from different angles** - people often need time to warm up. Asking about a topic twice, in different ways and at different moments, often leads to a deeper answer.
5. **Don't mention other participants** - it can break their privacy and influence the current participant's answers.
6. **Avoid leading questions** - "Was it easy to find the booking button?" suggests the answer. Ask "How did you find the booking button?" instead.

## The structure of a script

### 1. Introduction

The introduction sets a professional but friendly tone, makes participants comfortable, and collects basic information. It usually includes:

- **Welcome and thanks** - thank the participant for their time.
- **Consent to record** - explain that the session will be recorded so the team can review it, and ask for permission.
- **Basic information** - ask a few simple questions about the participant and their relationship with the topic.
- **"This is not a test"** - remind participants that you're testing the design, not them, and that there are no wrong answers.
- **Purpose** - briefly explain why you're collecting this data and how it will be used.
- **Questions** - invite participants to ask anything before you start.

### 2. Tasks

**Usability tasks** are the activities participants try to complete with your prototype while you observe. Calling them "activities" instead of "tasks" can make them feel less like an exam. Good tasks:

- **Come from your research goals** - each task tests something you want to learn.
- **Are specific** - "Book machine 2 for Thursday at 19:00", not "Try the app".
- **Ask for action** - participants should do something, not just look.
- **Don't give hints** - avoid words that appear on the button they need to press.

Each task can have **follow-up questions** about the participant's experience.

### 3. Wrap-up

At the end, ask any clarifying questions, stop the recording, and thank the participant again.

### What makes prompts and questions good?

- **Open-ended** - they invite action, discussion, and reflection instead of short answers.
- **Objective** - they don't give clues, lead to particular answers, or reveal your opinions.
- **Goal-oriented** - they connect directly to your research goals and KPIs.

## Example: the WashSlot script

### Introduction

- "Thank you for coming. Before we start, may we record the screen and audio of this session? Only the project team will see the recording."
- "We're testing an early version of an app, not you. There are no right or wrong answers, and you can't hurt our feelings, because honest feedback helps us improve the app."
- "Please think aloud as you go: tell us what you're looking at, what you expect, and what surprises you."
- "Feel free to ask questions at any time."
- **Basic questions** - How often do you do laundry? How do you usually book a machine today? Which devices do you use most?

### Activities

1. "Imagine you want to wash your clothes this Thursday at 19:00. Please use the app to arrange that."
   - *Follow-up:* "How did that go? What was easy, and what was difficult?"
2. "You'd like to know when your laundry is ready, without going downstairs. Please set that up."
   - *Follow-up:* "Tell me what you expected to happen."
3. "Find out what to do if someone else's laundry is still in the machine when your time starts."
   - *Follow-up:* "How confident are you in the answer you found?"
4. "Your plans changed. Please change your Thursday booking to Friday."
   - *Follow-up:* "How did you feel during this activity?"
5. "Overall, how would you describe the app to a neighbor?"

### Wrap-up

- Ask the participant to fill in the short SUS questionnaire.
- "Is there anything else you'd like to tell us?"
- Stop the recording, thank the participant, and hand over the incentive.

::: tip Key takeaway
The script keeps every session consistent and complete. Start with a friendly introduction and consent, give specific, action-based tasks that don't contain hints, ask open-ended and neutral follow-up questions, and end by thanking the participant.
:::
