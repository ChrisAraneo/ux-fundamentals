# Empathy maps

## What is an empathy map?

An **empathy map** is a simple chart that summarizes what designers have learned about a user or a type of user. It turns long interview notes into short pieces of information that the whole team can understand at a glance.

A classic empathy map has the user's name in the center and four sections around it:

| Says | Thinks |
| --- | --- |
| **Does** | **Feels** |

- **Says** - what the user said out loud, written as direct quotes.
- **Thinks** - what the user seems to think, based on what they said and how they said it.
- **Does** - what the user does: actions, habits, and behaviors they describe or show.
- **Feels** - the emotions the user expresses or shows, such as stress, joy, or guilt.

## How to create an empathy map

Start from an interview transcript, a recording, or detailed notes, then fill in the map step by step:

1. **Add the user's name** - this helps you find the original interview later and keeps different maps apart.
2. **Fill in "says"** - copy direct quotes word for word. Don't rephrase them, because a summary can accidentally change the meaning. Watch for problems the user mentions several times, because repeated topics often point to major pain points.
3. **Fill in "thinks"** - summarize the user's thoughts, including those they didn't say directly but showed through tone or body language. Be careful to make **inferences** (conclusions supported by what you saw and heard), not **assumptions** (guesses based on your own beliefs). If something seems contradictory, ask the user.
4. **Fill in "does"** - list the actions and routines the user described, including workarounds they use to deal with problems.
5. **Fill in "feels"** - list the emotions the user showed or described. It is fine if this overlaps with "thinks". If the user didn't mention feelings, ask in the interview: "How does that make you feel?"

### Inference vs. assumption

Imagine Tomasz says: "By the time I get downstairs, someone has usually taken the machine."

- **Assumption** - "Tomasz wants a bigger laundry room." He never said anything like that.
- **Inference** - "Tomasz wants to know whether a machine is free before he carries his basket downstairs." This follows directly from his words.

## Example: Tomasz's empathy map

The WashSlot designer interviews Tomasz, a single father who works from home. Here is his one-user empathy map:

### Says

- "By the time I get downstairs, someone has usually taken the machine."
- "I've carried the same basket up and down three times in one evening."
- "The paper list is useless. Half the names are crossed out and nobody sticks to it."
- "I'd pay a little extra if I could just know when a machine is free."

### Thinks

- Wants to know whether a machine is free before going downstairs.
- Would like to plan laundry around work calls and the children's activities.
- Feels the current system rewards whoever gets there first.
- Believes some neighbors don't respect the rules.

### Does

- Does laundry almost every day because of the children.
- Checks the laundry room several times a day, sometimes during work breaks.
- Leaves a note on his laundry when he has to go back upstairs.

### Feels

- Frustrated by wasted trips to the basement.
- Stressed when laundry interrupts work calls.
- Embarrassed when his clothes are left in a machine and a neighbor complains.
- Annoyed with neighbors who ignore the paper sheet.

## Types of empathy maps

There are two types of empathy maps:

1. **One-user empathy map** - based on a single interview. It helps you understand one person in depth, like Tomasz's map above.
2. **Aggregated empathy map** - combines several one-user maps of people who said, thought, did, or felt similar things. It represents a **user segment**, a group of users with similar needs or behaviors.

To create an aggregated map, first create one-user maps for every interview. Then group the people who share similar patterns and combine their maps. Each aggregated map should represent one segment. For example, WashSlot residents who do laundry every day (busy families) form a different segment from residents who do it once a week and rarely have problems.

Aggregated maps help you spot themes across many users, which prepares you for the next steps: identifying pain points and creating personas.

::: tip Key takeaway
An empathy map organizes what you learned about a user into four sections: says, thinks, does, and feels. Use direct quotes, make inferences instead of assumptions, and combine similar one-user maps into aggregated maps that represent whole user segments.
:::
