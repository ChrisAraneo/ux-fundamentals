# Problem and hypothesis statements

## From empathize to define

So far, you have collected a lot of information about your users: interview notes, empathy maps, pain points, personas, user stories, and journey maps. In the **define** phase, you turn all of this into a clear focus. Two questions guide this phase:

1. **Which user needs or problems are the most important to solve?**
2. **How might the design solve them in a way that creates real value for users?**

Three tools help you answer these questions: problem statements, hypothesis statements, and [value propositions](./11-value-propositions).

## Problem statements

A **problem statement** is a clear, short description of a user need that the design should address. You already saw the basic form in [Chapter 1](../foundations/08-empathize-and-define). Here is the version most UX teams use:

> **[Name]** is a/an **[short description of the user]** who needs **[need]** because **[insight]**.

The **insight** explains *why* the user has this need. It should be an **actionable insight**, something the design can actually do something about.

### What makes a problem statement strong?

Check your problem statement against three criteria:

- **Human-centered** - it focuses on a specific persona and their needs, not on technology or business goals.
- **Broad enough** - it leaves room for creativity and doesn't prescribe a specific solution.
- **Narrow enough** - it describes a problem that a design can realistically solve.

### What does a problem statement give the team?

A good problem statement helps the team:

- **Set a goal** - everyone knows exactly which need to focus on.
- **Understand constraints** - it shows what currently stops users from meeting their need.
- **Define deliverables** - it suggests what the team will need to produce, such as a booking feature or a prototype.
- **Measure success** - it hints at how you will know the problem is solved.

### The 5 Ws and H

Before writing a problem statement, make sure you understand the problem fully. The **5 Ws and H** framework is a set of six questions that help:

- **Who** - who is experiencing the problem?
- **What** - what are the pain points you're trying to solve?
- **Where** - where is the user when the problem happens? Physical context matters.
- **When** - when does the problem occur: at the start of a process, at the end, every day?
- **Why** - why does the problem matter, and how does it affect the user's life?
- **How** - how do users currently try to reach their goal?

*WashSlot example for Tomasz:*

- **Who** - a single father who works from home.
- **What** - he doesn't know whether a machine is free before carrying laundry downstairs.
- **Where** - at home on the third floor, often between work calls.
- **When** - several times a week, usually in the late afternoon when the laundry room is busiest.
- **Why** - wasted trips cost him time and interrupt his work.
- **How** - today he walks down to check, or relies on the unreliable paper sheet.

The resulting problem statement:

> Tomasz is a busy parent working from home who needs to know which machines are free before going to the laundry room because wasted trips interrupt his work and family time.

## Hypothesis statements

Once you know the problem, you can start thinking about the solution. A **hypothesis statement** puts into words your best informed guess at a solution. It moves you from the define phase toward ideation. It's a guess, so expect to test it and change it as you learn more.

Hypothesis statements don't have one fixed formula, but two formats are common.

### The "if... then..." format

> If **[user/action]**, then **[outcome]**.

This format focuses directly on the user. *Example:* If Tomasz can see the live status of every machine in the WashSlot app, then he will only go downstairs when a machine is free.

### The "we believe..." format

> We believe that **[solution]** for **[user]** will **[outcome]**.

This format adds the team's perspective while keeping the user in focus. *Example:* We believe that end-of-cycle alerts with vibration for residents like Grace will reduce the number of conflicts over forgotten laundry.

You may write several hypotheses for one problem. That's normal, because every problem has many possible solutions. Your job is to find the ones that work best for your users.

### What makes a hypothesis statement strong?

A strong hypothesis statement names:

- **A specific action** - what the solution lets the user do.
- **A desired outcome** - the result that will show whether the solution meets the user's need.

## Problem and hypothesis together

The two statements work as a pair: the hypothesis should mirror the problem. Here is a pair for Kofi:

- **Problem** - Kofi is a student working evening shifts who needs to do laundry late at night because his work schedule leaves no other free time, but the house rules and sign-up sheet are only in the local language, so he isn't sure what is allowed.
- **Hypothesis** - If Kofi can book a late-evening slot in an app that shows the house rules in his language, then he can do laundry with confidence and without conflicts with neighbors.

::: tip Key takeaway
A problem statement describes who the user is, what they need, and why, in a way that is human-centered, broad enough, and narrow enough. A hypothesis statement is your informed guess at how to solve it. Use the 5 Ws and H to understand the problem first.
:::
