# Moderating a usability study

Participants give the best feedback when they feel comfortable, respected, and free to be honest. As a moderator, your job is to create that atmosphere and keep the participant at the center of every step.

## Before the study begins

### Make participants comfortable

- **Start with small talk** - ask how their day is going, whether it was easy to get there or to join the call, or what they like to do in their free time. Adapt the warm-up to your participants; with children, for example, a short game works better.
- **Say thank you** - make it clear how much you appreciate their time. You can also say thank you with a small gift at the end.
- **Ask for honesty** - explain that you're there to improve the design, so criticism is welcome and won't hurt anyone's feelings. Problems they find will probably affect many other people too.
- **Prepare the space** - make sure the room is comfortable and quiet.

### Choose the right setting

- **Usability lab** - a room with equipment for testing, such as screen recording, devices, and sometimes eye-tracking tools that show where people look.
- **Participant's home or a public place** - find a spot with as few distractions as possible.
- **Online** - tell participants what you're recording (their face, voice, or screen) and that the recordings serve only this study.

### Handle the paperwork

- **NDA** - participants usually sign a [non-disclosure agreement](./07-privacy-and-ethics), because they'll see unreleased designs. Present it in a calm, friendly way. A fair, clearly explained NDA can actually increase trust, which matters especially for people from communities that have often been treated unfairly in research.
- **Consent to record** - participants must agree to the session being recorded, whether by notes, screen recording, video, or audio.
- **Minors** - if participants are children, their parents or guardians must give consent.

### Explain what will happen

Give participants a short overview of the session and ask: "Does that sound okay to you?" Check in with them whenever you change activities.

### Set the ground rules

- **Think aloud** - ask participants to use the **think-aloud method**: to say (or sign, or type) what they're thinking while they work, for example what they're looking for, what they expect, and what surprises them. Give an example of what that sounds like.
- **They're not being tested** - remind them that if they can't complete a task, it's a problem with the design, not with them. Nobody should leave a study feeling they failed.

### Check your own emotions

Before the session, take a moment to notice your own mood. Stress, tiredness, or excitement can influence how you ask questions and react. Keeping your emotions neutral, and noting when they weren't, helps you see later where you might have influenced the results.

## During the study

### Ask open-ended questions

Ask questions that can't be answered with "yes" or "no". After a task, ask "How did that go?" instead of "Did you like it?"

### Ask follow-up questions

If you're not sure what a participant means, don't guess. Ask "Tell me more about that" or "Can you say more about what you expected?"

### Summarize, but only at the end

If you're still unsure after follow-up questions, say in your own words what you understood, and let the participant correct or confirm it: "So you expected to choose the day first, before the machine. Is that right?" Use this only after open questions, because summarizing too early can lead participants toward your interpretation.

### Stay neutral and patient

Let participants find their own way, even when they struggle. Don't point at the screen or hint at the answer. Silence is fine; give people time to think.

*WashSlot example:* during activity 1, a participant stares at the booking screen for 20 seconds. The designer stays quiet. Then the participant says: "I'm looking for a calendar, but I only see times for today." This sentence is worth much more than any hint would have been.

::: tip Key takeaway
Before the study, build rapport, thank participants, handle consent and NDAs, explain the plan, introduce thinking aloud, and remind people they're not being tested. During the study, ask open-ended and follow-up questions, summarize only when needed, and stay neutral and patient.
:::
