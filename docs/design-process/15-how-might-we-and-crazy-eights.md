# How might we and Crazy Eights

This page explains two popular ideation exercises in more detail than [Chapter 1](../foundations/09-ideate). "How might we" works with words and careful thinking. Crazy Eights works with quick drawings and pure creativity. Good problem solving needs both kinds of thinking.

## How might we

### What is "How might we"?

**"How might we" (HMW)** is an exercise that turns problems into opportunities for design. You take a problem statement and rewrite it as many questions that begin with "How might we...?". Then you use those questions to spark ideas.

Each word in the phrase has a purpose:

- **How** - suggests that there is no answer yet and invites many possible ideas.
- **Might** - reminds you that every idea is a possible solution, not the only one.
- **We** - shows that coming up with ideas is a team effort.

### Find the right scope

A good HMW question is specific about the user's need but leaves room for different solutions. Compare these three questions for WashSlot:

- **Too broad** - "How might we improve laundry?" It gives no direction.
- **Too narrow** - "How might we send Grace a push notification with a vibration 10 minutes before her cycle ends on Tuesdays?" It already contains the solution.
- **Just right** - "How might we make sure residents know when their laundry is ready, even if they can't hear the machine?"

### Ways to write HMW questions

Start with a well-defined problem statement, then look at it from different angles. For example, take this problem statement:

> Grace is a retired resident with a hearing impairment who needs to know when her laundry is done because she can't hear the machine's signal and her neighbors get annoyed when her clothes block the machine.

You can reframe it in many ways:

- **Amplify the good** - How might we turn collecting laundry into a moment of friendly contact between neighbors?
- **Explore the opposite** - How might we make the laundry come to Grace instead of Grace going to the laundry?
- **Change the status quo** - How might we remove the need to collect laundry at a specific time?
- **Break it into pieces** - How might we let Grace know the cycle is ending? How might we keep the machine available for the next person?
- **Remove the negative** - How might we make forgotten laundry stop being a problem for neighbors?
- **Turn negatives into positives** - How might we make the laundry room feel welcoming instead of stressful?
- **Question assumptions** - How might we design laundry without fixed time slots?
- **Compare with another experience** - How might we make finishing a laundry cycle feel like a restaurant pager telling you that your table is ready?
- **Use unexpected resources** - How might the smart lights in Grace's flat help her know that the laundry is done?

### Best practices for HMW

- **Be broad enough** - a good question can be answered in many ways.
- **Don't be too broad** - the question should still give the team a clear direction.
- **Write many drafts** - if a question doesn't produce useful ideas, rewrite it.
- **Be creative** - questions can be playful and surprising.
- **Write as many questions as you can** - more questions lead to more solutions.

## Crazy Eights

### What is Crazy Eights?

**Crazy Eights**, also called **rapid sketching**, is an exercise in which you sketch eight different ideas in eight minutes. The time pressure stops you from overthinking and pushes you past the obvious ideas.

### You don't need to be an artist

Sketches in ideation don't need to be beautiful. Basic shapes (squares, circles, triangles), lines, arrows, stick figures, and a few words are enough. If your handwriting is messy, write in capital letters, horizontally, so others can read it. Later you might sketch phone screens or web pages, but for now, simply draw what you can.

Why sketch by hand instead of on a computer? Because speed matters. Software can slow you down when your hand wants to move as fast as your thoughts.

### How to do Crazy Eights

1. **Prepare paper** - fold a large sheet of paper in half three times. When you unfold it, you have eight rectangles.
2. **Choose a pen** - a thick marker is best, because it stops you from drawing tiny details.
3. **Pick one problem** - use one problem statement or one HMW question for the whole exercise.
4. **Set a timer** - set it for eight minutes.
5. **Sketch** - spend about one minute on each rectangle and draw a different idea in each one.

If you still have ideas left when the timer rings, do another round.

### Crazy Eights in a group

The exercise is often done in teams, for example during the ideate phase of a [design sprint](../foundations/11-design-sprints). Everyone sketches on their own first. Then each person presents their two or three best ideas, or the whole team votes on favorites. The selected ideas are developed further in more detailed sketches. Five people produce 40 ideas in just eight minutes.

### WashSlot example

Using the HMW question "How might we make sure residents know when their laundry is ready, even if they can't hear the machine?", the designer sketches eight ideas:

1. **Phone alert** - a notification with a strong vibration.
2. **Light signal** - a colored lamp in the stairwell for each machine.
3. **Tablet screen** - a large screen in the elevator showing machine status.
4. **Countdown widget** - a home-screen widget showing time left.
5. **Neighbor buddy** - a system where neighbors can collect laundry for each other.
6. **Smart light** - Grace's living room lamp blinks when the cycle ends.
7. **Laundry lockers** - finished laundry goes into a locker, freeing the machine.
8. **Text message** - an SMS for residents without the app.

Several ideas (1, 4, and 8) end up in the first version of WashSlot. The others go into a list for future versions.

### Best practices for Crazy Eights

- **Warm up first** - for example, sketch your neighbor's face while keeping your eyes off the paper. It loosens up your hand and your mind.
- **Use one well-defined problem** - one problem statement or HMW question per round.
- **Don't judge your own ideas** - fill every rectangle, even with crazy ideas.
- **Don't judge others' ideas** - listen with an open mind when people present.
- **Invite a diverse group** - different roles, experiences, and abilities lead to more varied ideas.
- **Find a relaxed place** - a change of scenery helps creative thinking.
- **Don't be afraid of drawing** - stick figures are perfectly fine; what matters is that the idea is clear.

## Check ideas against your research

After a burst of creativity, step back and check your ideas against your personas and user journeys. An idea might work for one persona and fail for another. The stairwell lamp helps Grace, but the cooperative's budget might not allow it, and Kofi, who does laundry at midnight, might not want a blinking light in the stairwell.

If you realize you are missing important information, for example how many residents have smart lights at home, go back to research before moving on to prototypes. Telling the story of this process, from research to problem to ideas, also makes a strong section in a portfolio [case study](./01-ux-portfolio).

::: tip Key takeaway
"How might we" turns a problem statement into many questions that open up new perspectives. Crazy Eights produces eight quick sketches in eight minutes. Use both, without judging ideas too early, and then check the best ones against your personas and research.
:::
