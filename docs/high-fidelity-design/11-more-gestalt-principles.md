# More Gestalt principles

## A quick refresher

**Gestalt principles** describe how people automatically group elements, see patterns, and simplify what they see into meaningful wholes. In [Chapter 3](../wireframes-and-prototypes/08-gestalt-principles), you used three of them in wireframes:

- **Similarity** - items that look alike are understood to work alike.
- **Proximity** - items placed near each other look connected, and items far apart look separate.
- **Common region** - elements inside the same enclosed area seem to belong together.

In mockups, these principles become even more powerful, because you now have color, type, and shadows to work with. Gestalt principles also give you a useful way to step back from the details and check whether the whole design makes sense.

*WashSlot example:* all tappable elements in WashSlot use the deep teal accent: the "Book a slot" button, the selected day tab, and the "Confirm booking" button. Because of **similarity**, residents learn quickly that deep teal means "you can act here". Each booking in "My bookings" sits in its own card (**common region**), and inside each card, the machine, the day, and the time are grouped tightly together (**proximity**).

## Closure

**Closure** describes how people fill in missing parts when they look at an incomplete shape. If a circle has a small gap, you still see a circle. Your brain prefers complete, familiar forms.

Designers use closure in logos and icons, where a few lines are enough to suggest a whole object. It also explains why a row of cards that is cut off at the edge of the screen suggests that more cards continue beyond it.

*WashSlot example:* the WashSlot logo is a circle made of three arcs with gaps between them. People still see a round washing machine door, and the gaps make the logo feel lighter.

## Continuity

**Continuity** describes how elements arranged along a line or a curve seem more related than elements that aren't. Our eyes follow the line from one element to the next.

Continuity is useful for step-by-step processes, timelines, and related lists. It also helps in menus: if items are aligned in a clear vertical line, people move their eyes down the line without effort.

*WashSlot example:* the confirmation screen shows the booking as three steps connected by a thin line: "Booked", "Reminder 30 min before", and "Machine ready". The line leads the eye from the first step to the last.

## Symmetry and asymmetry

**Symmetry** means that the parts of a design mirror each other or are balanced around a center. Symmetrical layouts feel stable, calm, and orderly, and symmetrical elements are seen as belonging together.

**Asymmetry** is a lack of balance or equality. It isn't a mistake: an asymmetrical element breaks the pattern and draws attention, which makes it useful for highlighting something important or adding interest.

## Visual balance

**Visual balance** is the feeling that a design's visual weight is evenly distributed around its center of attention. A balanced design feels complete. You can balance a screen symmetrically, with similar elements on both sides, or asymmetrically, for example with a large element on one side and several smaller ones on the other.

*WashSlot example:* the home screen is mostly symmetrical: four equal cards in a column, centered title, and a full-width button. The one asymmetrical element is a small "1 free" badge at the right end of the "Today" section header, which draws attention exactly where the designer wants it.

## Step back: review your mockups at four levels

Even experienced designers stop regularly to review their progress. A useful checklist moves from small details to the big picture:

1. **Detail level** - check individual elements: typography, colors, and icons. Are they consistent everywhere? It's easy to use a slightly different shade or font size by accident.
2. **Page level** - look at each screen as a whole. Is it balanced and unified? Do the scale and proportion of the elements make sense?
3. **Function level** - check that each screen works. Is there a way forward and back on every screen? Does each screen have a clear button for its main action? Can users undo or cancel an action?
4. **App level** - zoom out in Figma to see all screens at once. Does the product look consistent? Do recurring elements, such as the navigation bar, stay in the same place on every screen?

*WashSlot example:* at the detail level, the designer finds that the "My bookings" title uses 26 px instead of the 28 px heading style, so they apply the text style. At the function level, they notice that the summary screen has no way to go back and change the machine, so they add a back arrow. At the app level, they see that the bottom navigation bar is 8 px higher on one screen and fix it.

::: tip Key takeaway
Similarity, proximity, and common region are even more useful in mockups. Closure, continuity, and symmetry add more ways to guide the eye, and visual balance makes a design feel complete. Review your mockups regularly at four levels: details, pages, functions, and the whole app.
:::
