# Language in research

## Why language matters

Language is your most important research tool. You use it to collect data, build trust with participants, report findings to your team, and represent users' voices. The words you choose affect the words participants choose. If you speak very formally, participants may become stiff and guarded. If you speak naturally and warmly, they're more likely to open up.

Every word in your questions, notes, and quotes must accurately represent what participants meant.

## Respect different ways of speaking

All languages and language varieties deserve equal respect. The same idea can be expressed differently depending on the region, community, or dialect. British English speakers take the *lift*, while American English speakers take the *elevator*. Many communities and dialects have their own words, pronunciations, and expressions.

If you're not sure what a participant meant, **ask them**. If you can't, ask colleagues or experts familiar with that language variety. It's your responsibility to clarify differences and to make sure participants' words are translated and reported accurately.

## Avoid language that excludes

Language can reveal hidden power imbalances. Aim to sound inviting, not commanding. Avoid **ableist language**, words that assume everyone has the same physical or mental abilities. For example, instead of "Let's walk through the study", you can say "Let's go through the study". The second phrase makes no assumptions about the participant's body.

Other ways to put people first:

- **Ask how people want to be addressed** - use participants' preferred pronouns, pronounce their names correctly, use the right titles, and ask about any other communication preferences.
- **Use people-first language** - describe the person before the condition, for example "a person who uses a wheelchair" rather than "a wheelchair-bound person". Some communities prefer identity-first language, such as "deaf person" or "disabled person", so when in doubt, ask.
- **Speak to the participant, not the interpreter** - when a participant works with an interpreter, look at the participant and address your questions directly to them.

Changing language habits takes time. You will make mistakes, and that's okay. Being aware of your words is already an important step toward inclusive research.

## Mind the context

The setting of a study also communicates something. Participants should feel that the relationship between you and them is equal. If you stand while they sit, or sit behind a large desk, the situation can feel like an exam. Sit at the same level and create a relaxed atmosphere.

Remember that communication norms differ between cultures. In some cultures, people wait for others to finish before speaking; in others, interrupting shows enthusiasm. One participant may find interruptions rude, while another sees them as a sign of engagement. Pay attention to how each participant prefers to communicate, and adapt to them.

::: tip Key takeaway
The language you use shapes the answers you get. Speak naturally, respect different language varieties, ask when something is unclear, avoid ableist and exclusive language, put people first, and create a setting where participants feel like equals.
