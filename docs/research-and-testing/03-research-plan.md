# The research plan

## Seven elements of a research plan

Planning is the first step of a research study. A **research plan** is a document that describes what you want to learn and how you'll learn it. It usually has seven elements:

1. **Project background** - why you're doing this research.
2. **Research goals** - what you want to learn.
3. **Research questions** - the specific questions the study should answer.
4. **Key performance indicators (KPIs)** - how you'll measure success.
5. **Methodology** - how the study will be carried out.
6. **Participants** - who will take part.
7. **Script** - what you'll say and ask during the sessions.

This page covers the first three elements. The others have their own pages: [KPIs](./04-kpis), [methodology and participants](./05-methodology-and-participants), and the [script](./06-research-script).

## The introduction

A research plan usually starts with a short introduction that contains:

- **Title** - a name that shows the focus and purpose of the study, for example whether you're creating something new or improving something that exists.
- **Author** - the name, role, and contact details of the person who wrote the plan, so others know whom to contact.
- **Stakeholders** - the people involved in or affected by the research, with their roles.
- **Date** - the date of the **most recent change**, not the creation date. Update it every time you change the plan, so everyone knows whether they're reading the current version.
- **Project background** - a short explanation of why the research is needed.
- **Research goals** - what you want to learn and how the results will influence design decisions.

## Project background

The **project background** explains what prompted the research. A few sentences are enough. It gets the whole team on the same page before the study starts, and it shows stakeholders that you understand why the research matters, which makes them more likely to trust and act on your results later.

When writing the background:

1. **Name the signals** - what showed that research was needed? Is there a problem to solve?
2. **Mention previous work** - what research or solutions have already been tried, and what did they teach you?
3. **Describe the expected insights** - what will you learn, and which decisions will depend on it? An **insight** is a finding about people that shows users or their needs in a new light.

Keep it short; a long background won't get read.

## Research goals

**Research goals** (sometimes called research objectives) describe what you want to learn from the study. They show the bigger picture and drive the entire study. To write them, ask: What design problems are we trying to solve for users and the business? How will the results affect our design decisions?

Research goals depend on when in the product life cycle the research happens:

- **Before design (foundational research)** - the goal is to understand whether there's a real need and **why or if** the product should be built.
- **During design (design research)** - the goal is to learn **how** to build the product and which features it needs.
- **After launch (post-launch research)** - the goal is to find out **whether the product works** as expected.

## Research questions

**Research questions** are the handful of questions the study should answer. They are **not** the questions you'll ask participants; those go into the script. Research questions guide the study and later become the main topics of your presentation.

Good research questions are:

- **Actionable** - it's clear how to find the answer, and you'll know when you've found it.
- **Specific** - they focus on a concrete aspect of the experience, so the data will be meaningful.
- **Neutral** - they don't assume an answer. "Why is booking so difficult?" assumes that it is difficult; "How do residents book a machine?" doesn't.
- **Clear about the type of data** - they show whether you need **quantitative** data (numbers, such as "how long", "how many") or **qualitative** data (observations about why and how things happen).

## Example: the WashSlot research plan introduction

- **Title** - WashSlot booking prototype: usability study.
- **Author** - the WashSlot UX designer, with an email address for questions.
- **Stakeholders** - the residents of the building, the housing cooperative's board, the building caretaker, and the developer who will build the app.
- **Date** - the date of the latest update.
- **Project background** - Residents currently book washing machines on a paper sheet in the laundry room, which often leads to wasted trips and conflicts. We have built a low-fidelity prototype of WashSlot, an app for checking and booking machines. Before development starts, we need to know whether residents can complete the main booking flow without help.
- **Research goal** - Find out whether residents can check machine availability and book a machine easily, and which parts of the flow cause difficulties.

### Research questions

1. How long does it take residents to book a free machine for a chosen day and time? *(quantitative)*
2. What difficulties do residents experience while choosing a machine and a time? *(qualitative)*

::: tip Key takeaway
A research plan has seven elements: project background, research goals, research questions, KPIs, methodology, participants, and script. Keep the background short, match your goals to the product stage, and write research questions that are actionable, specific, neutral, and clear about the type of data you need.
:::
