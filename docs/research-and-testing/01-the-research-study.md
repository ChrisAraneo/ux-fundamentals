# The research study

## What is a research study?

A **research study** (often just called a **study**) is a structured, step-by-step investigation of a group of users and what they need. It brings real-world context into the design process. Instead of guessing what people need, the team observes and asks them, then uses what it learns to make design decisions.

Research studies help close the gap between what a business *believes* users need and what users *actually* need, before the team spends a lot of time and money building the wrong thing.

## The four steps of a research study

Every study follows the same four steps:

1. **Plan the study** - describe why the research is needed, set goals, write the questions you want to answer, decide how you'll collect data, and choose participants. Good planning affects every later step, so take it seriously.
2. **Conduct the research** - collect data. This chapter focuses on the **usability study**, a method in which participants try to complete key tasks with your design while you observe.
3. **Analyze and synthesize the results** - find the meaning in the data. Look for patterns in numbers and recurring topics in what participants said and did, and figure out *why* things happened.
4. **Share and promote the insights** - present what you learned to the project's stakeholders and convince them to act on it.

**Project stakeholders** are the people involved in the project or affected by its results, for example other designers, managers, engineers, or a client. They need to understand your results and agree on what to do next. A good presentation of results usually covers four things: how you worked (the **method**), what came out (the **data**), what you concluded (the **conclusions**), and what you suggest doing (the **recommendations**). The more clearly your recommendations follow from your conclusions, the more likely stakeholders are to act on them.

## Research throughout the design process

Research is not a one-time event. You learned in [Chapter 1](../foundations/12-ux-research) that it happens before design (foundational research), during design (design research), and after launch (post-launch research). The studies in this chapter are **design research**: you test prototypes to find problems while they are still cheap to fix.

Testing prototypes saves time and money. Changing a wireframe takes minutes; changing a finished app can take weeks. And every round of feedback makes the final experience better.

## Research in the test phase

Even when a product is almost ready to launch, research continues. In the **test phase**, the team checks that the finished design really works. Changes at this stage should be small refinements; big changes belong in earlier prototype rounds. Common types of testing in this phase are:

- **Acceptance testing** - observing how real users react to the finished product. **User acceptance testing (UAT)** checks that the product fulfills the purpose it was built for.
- **Quality assurance (QA) testing** - checking the product for errors and making sure everything works as intended. When something is broken, for example a button that opens the wrong screen, the team files a **bug report** for the developers.
- **Accessibility evaluation** - checking that every part of the product meets accessibility standards. It is often part of QA testing.

### How test-phase research is organized

- **Participants** - recruit people who match your target users. Some online services offer ready pools of test participants; you can also recruit people yourself.
- **Location** - tests can run in a lab, where the team watches participants in person, or online, where sessions are recorded for later review.
- **Questionnaires** - participants can complete specific tasks and then answer questions about them, followed by general questions about the whole experience.

*WashSlot example:* a test-phase questionnaire might ask a resident to book a machine for Saturday morning and then ask: "How was choosing the time? What would you change?" General questions could follow, such as "How did moving between screens feel?" and "Did you find everything you needed to make your booking?"

::: tip Key takeaway
A research study has four steps: plan the study, conduct the research, analyze and synthesize the results, and share the insights. Research runs through the whole design process, from prototypes to acceptance, QA, and accessibility testing of the finished product.
:::
