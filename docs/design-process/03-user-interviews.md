# User interviews

Interviews are one of the most valuable ways to empathize with users. This page explains how to plan an interview, find the right participants, and run the conversation from the first "hello" to the final "thank you". For an overview of other research methods, see [UX research](../foundations/12-ux-research) in Chapter 1.

## Plan the interview

Good interviews start long before you meet anyone. A simple interview plan has three parts: research goals, participant characteristics, and interview questions.

### 1. Research goals

**Research goals** describe what you want to learn. They keep the interview focused and later guide your design decisions. To write them, ask yourself: Who are my users? What problem is my product trying to solve? What do I still not know about my users' experience?

*WashSlot example:* the designer writes two goals:

1. **Understand routines** - learn how residents plan and organize their laundry during a typical week.
2. **Find frustrations** - identify what annoys or worries residents about using the shared laundry room today.

### 2. Participant characteristics

Next, decide **who** you want to talk to. The aim is a **representative sample**: a small group of people whose characteristics reflect the larger group of real users. Think about characteristics that might shape how people experience your product, such as age, job, schedule, language, and disability.

Make sure your sample includes groups that research often overlooks. Leaving them out leads to products that don't work for them.

*WashSlot example:* the designer looks for residents of different ages, including at least one older resident, one resident who doesn't speak the local language fluently, one resident with a disability, and residents with very different daily schedules.

### 3. Interview questions

Finally, write the questions you will ask. Using the same core questions with every participant makes the interviews easier to compare. Good interview questions are:

- **Relevant** - connected to your research goals and the problem you are exploring.
- **Open-ended** - they invite stories, not just "yes" or "no".
- **Neutral** - they don't suggest a "correct" answer or assume how the participant feels.
- **Short and clear** - easy to understand on the first try, in everyday language.
- **Ready for follow-up** - they leave room for prompts like "Tell me more about that" or "Why do you think that happened?"

### Leading vs. neutral questions

Compare these two questions:

- **Leading** - "Is the paper sign-up sheet annoying because it gets wet, or because people ignore it?"
- **Neutral** - "How do you decide when to do your laundry?"

The first question assumes the sheet is annoying and offers only two possible reasons. The second lets the participant tell their own story, which might reveal something you never expected.

## Find and recruit participants

### Screener surveys

A **screener survey** is a short questionnaire that helps you check whether potential participants match your research needs before you invite them. It often asks about **demographics**, the characteristics of a person or group, such as age, location, or occupation.

Demographic questions can be sensitive. Ask only what you really need, make such questions optional, use respectful and inclusive wording, and explain why you are asking. For example: "We ask this to make sure we hear from a diverse group of people."

### Where to find participants

Depending on your budget, time, and product, you can recruit participants in several ways:

- **Your personal network** - friends, family, and colleagues who match your target users. This is a good start for learners, but be careful, because people who know you may be too kind.
- **Existing users** - if the product already has users, the company can invite some of them.
- **Online communities** - social media, online groups, or dedicated platforms that connect researchers with participants. Always check the group's rules before posting.
- **Hallway testing** - asking people in a public place, such as a café or a park, to answer a few questions. It's quick and cheap, but the people you meet may not match your target users.
- **Recruiting agencies** - companies that find suitable participants for a fee. They save time and can reach diverse groups.

*WashSlot example:* the designer puts a notice in the laundry room and the building's newsletter, and asks the cooperative to forward an invitation by email, so residents without social media can also take part.

## Prepare for the interview

1. **Write a script** - list your questions in order. Treat it as a guide, not a rigid rule; you can follow interesting topics when they come up.
2. **Gather supplies** - a printed list of questions, a notebook or laptop, and recording equipment if the participant agrees to be recorded. Test new tools in advance.
3. **Have a backup plan** - for online interviews, make sure the participant can use the video tool, and keep a phone number in case the connection fails.
4. **Learn about the participant** - review their screener answers or any information they shared, so you can personalize the conversation.
5. **Practice** - rehearse your questions with a colleague or in front of a mirror. Practice also shows whether the interview is too long.

## Run the interview

### Meet the participant

First impressions matter. Your job is to help the participant feel relaxed:

- **Build rapport** - start with friendly small talk, such as asking how their day is going.
- **Say thank you** - show that you value their time and opinions.
- **Handle the formalities** - explain how the interview works and ask for consent, for example to record the session.
- **Start with easy questions** - begin with simple background questions before moving to the main topic.
- **Remove the pressure** - make it clear that there are no right or wrong answers.

### Ask your questions

Speak clearly, stay professional and neutral no matter what the participant says, and show that you are listening by nodding and keeping eye contact. If you get a short answer, ask a follow-up question. Questions that start with "why", "how", or "tell me about" usually lead to the richest answers.

### Take notes

Conversations move fast, and memory is unreliable. While listening, capture:

- **Memorable quotes** - write down striking sentences word for word. Quotes show how users really think and feel, and they are very useful later in empathy maps and presentations.
- **Observations** - note mood, facial expressions, body language, and behavior. Also note outside factors, such as noise or interruptions, that might influence the answers.
- **Recordings** - if the participant agrees, record the session so you can check details later.

In professional teams, one person often leads the interview while another takes notes. When you work alone, you will do both.

### End the interview

When you have asked all your questions, give the participant a chance to add anything else. People often share important thoughts at the very end, once they feel comfortable. Then thank them again and give them any promised reward, such as a voucher. You want every participant to leave feeling that their opinion truly mattered.

::: tip Key takeaway
A good interview starts with a plan: clear research goals, a representative group of participants, and relevant, open-ended, neutral questions. During the interview, make people comfortable, listen actively, take careful notes, and end with gratitude.
:::
