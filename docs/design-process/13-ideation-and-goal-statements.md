# Ideation and goal statements

## What is ideation?

**Ideation** means producing as many ideas as possible about a problem **while holding back any judgment**. It's the third phase of design thinking, where you move from understanding the problem to looking for solutions. You saw a short introduction in [Chapter 1](../foundations/09-ideate); this page goes deeper.

Two parts of the definition matter most:

- **A broad set of ideas** - ideation is about quantity. The more ideas you have, the better your chances of finding a great one.
- **No judgment** - every idea is welcome at first. Ideas that sound silly can lead to the most innovative solutions. Not long ago, a phone that had no physical keyboard sounded like a strange idea to many people.

## Prepare for ideation

Ideation works best when the first two phases are done:

- **You have empathized with users** - you know who you are designing for, thanks to interviews, empathy maps, personas, user stories, and journey maps.
- **You have defined the problem** - a clear [problem statement](./10-problem-and-hypothesis-statements) makes sure everyone is solving the same problem.

## How to ideate effectively

1. **Create a creative environment** - choose a comfortable space, ideally different from your usual desk. For online sessions, find a quiet spot without clutter or distractions.
2. **Set a time limit** - give yourself enough time to explore, but also decide when brainstorming stops and evaluation starts.
3. **Build a diverse team** - people with different backgrounds, abilities, genders, and roles (designers, researchers, writers, engineers) bring different ideas.
4. **Capture everything** - write every idea on a sticky note or whiteboard. Aim for quantity over quality.
5. **Question the obvious** - challenge "the way things have always been done". The first ideas are usually the most obvious and the least creative.
6. **Take a break** - after the session, step away before you evaluate the ideas with fresh eyes.

## Why explore many ideas?

It might feel faster to pick the first good idea, but exploring many ideas has three important advantages:

- **Constraints will remove some ideas** - budget, deadlines, and technology will rule out some of your favorites. A long list means you still have good options left.
- **Equity** - the first idea often works for people like you. Many ideas give you a better chance of finding one that works for everyone.
- **Users decide** - the best idea is the one that works for users, not the one the team likes most. With several ideas, you can let users test them and show you the answer.

*WashSlot example:* for the problem "residents don't know if a machine is free", the team lists many ideas: a live status page, a camera in the laundry room, colored lights on each machine visible from the stairwell, a booking calendar, a queue system, a text message when a machine becomes free, a shared family calendar, and even a robot that carries baskets. The robot is unrealistic, but it leads to a practical idea: a laundry cart in the elevator.

## Evaluate ideas

Once you have many ideas, evaluate them with clear reasons. Three common criteria are:

1. **Feasible** - is the idea technically possible to build with the available skills and tools?
2. **Desirable** - does it solve the user problem you are focusing on?
3. **Viable** - does it make sense for the business, for example within the budget?

An idea that meets all three criteria is a strong candidate.

## Keep the business in mind

UX designers design for users **and** for the organization behind the product. During ideation, think about:

- **Voice and tone** - every brand has a personality. Compare a booking confirmation that says "All set! Machine 2 is yours at 18:00" with one that says "Reservation ID 4471 confirmed." The first sounds human and friendly, the second sounds cold. Both work, but they create very different experiences, so match the tone to the brand.
- **Business goals** - good design often helps both sides. A clear, easy checkout helps users and increases sales.
- **Competitors** - knowing what competitors do well and badly helps you make better decisions. You will learn how to study them on the [competitive audits](./14-competitive-audits) page.

## Let research guide your ideas

Ideas shouldn't come from nowhere or from your personal preferences. Go back to your research tools while you ideate:

- **Empathy maps** - recall what users said, thought, did, and felt, so your ideas respond to real emotions.
- **Personas** - remind you who you are designing for and what goals they have.
- **User stories** - help you focus on the most critical needs.
- **User journey maps** - show the difficult moments where ideas are needed most.
- **Problem statements** - keep everyone focused on the same problem.

Research doesn't stop when ideation starts. Researchers and designers continue to test ideas with users, often and early, and keep learning after the product launches. If you realize during ideation that you are missing important information, go back and do more research.

## Goal statements

At the end of ideation, you move from the **problem** to a **solution**. A **goal statement** describes that solution in one or two sentences: what the product does and how it benefits users.

### The goal statement template

> Our **[product]** lets **[audience]** **[do a specific action]**, which helps them by **[positive impact]**. We'll know it works when **[measurable result]**.

A strong goal statement covers five elements:

1. **Product** - the product you are designing.
2. **Action** - the specific action the product lets users perform.
3. **Audience** - who the action mainly affects.
4. **Impact** - how the action helps them.
5. **Measurement** - how you will measure whether it works. This keeps the goal realistic and testable.

### Where the parts come from

The easiest source is your problem statement. If you don't have one, you can also take the **who** from your persona, the **what** from your user story, the **why** from your empathy map and journey map, and the **how** from your brainstorming.

### WashSlot example

- **Problem statement** - Tomasz is a busy parent working from home who needs to know which machines are free before going to the laundry room because wasted trips interrupt his work and family time.
- **Goal statement** - Our WashSlot app lets residents who share a laundry room check machine availability and book a slot in advance, which helps them by saving unnecessary trips and reducing conflicts between neighbors. We'll know it works when weekly bookings grow and residents report fewer wasted trips in a monthly survey.

::: tip Key takeaway
Ideation means generating many ideas without judgment, then evaluating them by whether they are feasible, desirable, and viable. Keep both users and the business in mind, let research guide your ideas, and summarize the chosen direction in a measurable goal statement.
:::
