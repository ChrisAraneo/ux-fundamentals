# Building a UX portfolio

## What is a portfolio?

A **portfolio** is a curated selection of your projects that demonstrates your skills. Painters, photographers, and architects have portfolios, and so do UX designers. When you apply for a job, a portfolio lets recruiters and hiring managers see your skills instead of just reading about them.

Years ago, portfolios were physical folders full of printed work. Today, a UX portfolio is almost always a **website**, because it is easy to update, easy to share with a link, and works on any device.

### Why start now?

You don't need finished projects to start a portfolio. Building it early has two advantages:

- **You add work as you go** - every persona, journey map, or sketch you create while learning can become part of a project in your portfolio.
- **The website itself is a design project** - a portfolio with good navigation and a clear structure proves that you understand UX. A confusing portfolio sends the opposite message.

## Personal brand

Your **personal brand** is the impression people form of you as a designer, shaped by your personality, skills, and values and by how you show them publicly. It is the first impression people get when they open your portfolio. A good personal brand quickly answers the questions "Who is this designer?" and "What makes their work interesting?"

Your brand shows through your writing style, the colors and fonts you choose, the projects you include, and the way you describe your role in them.

## Tools for building a portfolio website

You don't need to know how to code. **Website builders** such as Wix, Squarespace, Webflow, or Framer offer ready-made templates and a **WYSIWYG** editor ("what you see is what you get"), where you edit the page visually, similar to a text editor. Some builders are simpler, while others give you more control but expect a bit of technical knowledge.

If you can code, you can also build the site yourself. Just remember that recruiters want to see your **design** work, not your programming skills, so keep the focus on your projects.

Whatever tool you choose, consider buying your own **domain**, the address of your website (for example `yourname.design`). A personal domain looks more professional than an address that contains the name of the website builder.

## Elements of a portfolio website

Plan your website around these elements:

1. **Your name and logo** - put your name at the top of every page so visitors always know whose work they are viewing. A simple personal logo, such as stylized initials, is optional.
2. **Navigation** - keep the menu short and clear. From any page, one click should take visitors home or to the next project.
3. **Work samples** - your projects are the heart of the portfolio, so choose a layout that lets them stand out.
4. **About me** - a page about your interests, experience, and what drives you as a designer.
5. **Contact information** - how to reach you by email, where to download your resume, and your professional profiles, such as LinkedIn. Use a professional-looking email address.
6. **Color scheme** - a small set of colors, for example one main color and one accent color, that reflects your personality without distracting from your work.

A quick paper sketch of your home page and "About me" page helps you choose a fitting template later. While sketching, ask yourself: What goes on the home page? Which elements belong together? How many pages do I need, and what should they be called?

## Best practices

1. **Tell a story** - present each project with a beginning (the problem), a middle (your process: research, sketches, wireframes), and an end (the final design and what changed because of it).
2. **Be concise** - use short texts and let images of your work do most of the talking. Link to longer documents if someone wants more detail.
3. **Keep navigation simple** - visitors should never feel lost or have to search for your contact page.
4. **Go beyond the template** - replace every placeholder text and image, and adapt the layout to your brand. A template should be a starting point, not the finished site.
5. **Show variety** - include about three to six projects that show different skills, for example a mobile app, a responsive website, and a research-heavy project.
6. **Feature case studies** - show the full process behind each project, not only polished screens.
7. **Make it responsive** - the site should adapt to any screen size. Many recruiters will open your link on a phone.
8. **Test it** - check your site on different devices and browsers, and ask friends to try it. Misaligned text on mobile is an easy way to lose a recruiter's attention.

Remember that there is no single "right" portfolio. Look at many portfolios of other designers, note what you like and why, and use those observations as inspiration rather than templates to copy.

## Case studies

A **case study** walks the reader through one project from the first research to the final design. It is the most important part of a UX portfolio, because it shows how you think, not just what you made.

### What does a case study answer?

Readers of a case study look for answers to four questions:

- **What was the problem?** - who the users were and what made their experience difficult.
- **What did you do?** - your role, your process, and the methods you used.
- **What did you learn?** - key research insights and how they changed your design decisions.
- **What was the result?** - the final solution, why you believe it was the right one, and what you would do differently next time.

Useful elements to include are the project name and duration, your role and personal contribution, the goal, the target audience, research findings, sketches and wireframes, test results, the final design, and your reflections.

### Respect confidentiality

When you work for a company, you may sign a **non-disclosure agreement (NDA)**, a contract in which you promise not to share confidential information such as unreleased designs. Never put protected work in your portfolio. If you want to talk about such a project, describe your process in general terms or recreate the work with invented content, and ask your employer what you are allowed to show.

::: tip Key takeaway
A UX portfolio is a website that tells the story of your work. Start it early, keep it simple and responsive, show a variety of projects, and present each one as a case study that explains the problem, your process, and the result.
:::
