# Bias in UX research

## What is bias?

A **bias** is a tendency to favor or reject something based on limited information. It's like forming an opinion about someone before you really get to know them.

Our brains create mental shortcuts to process information quickly. These shortcuts are useful in everyday life, but they can lead to wrong conclusions. **Everyone has biases, and most of them are unconscious.** We can't remove them completely, but we can learn to notice them and reduce their influence. In UX research, this is critical, because biased research leads to products that don't fit real users.

## Common biases in UX research

### Confirmation bias

**Confirmation bias** happens when you look for evidence that proves what you already believe. Because you think you know the answer, you notice information that supports it and ignore information that doesn't.

*Example:* a designer believes users want a dark theme. During interviews, they remember the two people who mentioned it and forget the eight who never did.

*How to reduce it:* ask open-ended questions, listen actively without sharing your own opinion, and include a large and diverse group of participants.

### False consensus bias

**False consensus bias** is the assumption that other people think the same way you do. In UX, it often means overestimating how many people will like your idea or design.

*Example:* a designer who loves keyboard shortcuts assumes "everyone" uses them and hides important actions behind shortcuts only.

*How to reduce it:* write down your assumptions so you can test them, and research with larger and more varied groups of people.

### Recency bias

**Recency bias** is the tendency to remember the last thing you heard most clearly. After an interview, you are likely to remember the final comments better than what was said at the beginning.

*How to reduce it:* take detailed notes or record interviews (with permission), so you can review the whole conversation.

### Primacy bias

**Primacy bias** is the opposite: you remember the **first** thing, or the first participant, most strongly, because first impressions are powerful.

*How to reduce it:* take careful notes, run every interview in the same way so they are easy to compare, and space interviews out so you have time to process each one.

### Implicit bias

**Implicit bias** (also called unconscious bias) is the set of attitudes and stereotypes we connect with groups of people without being aware of it. In UX research, it can cause us to interview only people similar to ourselves, or to ask people from underrepresented groups questions that are insensitive or offensive.

*How to reduce it:* reflect on your own behavior, ask colleagues to point out your blind spots, and actively recruit diverse participants. A tool like the [Implicit Association Test](https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/takeatest.html) from Project Implicit can help you discover some of your own implicit associations. It doesn't measure all biases, but it can be a valuable starting point for reflection.

### Sunk cost fallacy

The **sunk cost fallacy** is the feeling that the more time or money you have invested in something, the harder it is to stop, even when stopping is the right decision. "Sunk cost" is the effort that is already spent and can't be recovered.

*Example:* you spent two weeks designing a feature, and then research shows that users don't need it. It's tempting to keep going, because stopping feels like wasting those two weeks.

*How to reduce it:* split the project into smaller phases and plan checkpoints where you consciously decide whether to continue, change direction, or stop.

## Biases that affect participants

Some biases come from the way research is run, and they change how participants answer.

### Framing effect

The **framing effect** happens when people's answers depend on how a question is worded. If you ask "What do you like about the **new, simplified** menu?", most people will say something positive, because the question already suggests that the menu is good.

*Better question:* "Describe your experience using the menu."

### Bandwagon effect

The **bandwagon effect** is the tendency to go along with the opinion of the group. In a group interview, the first confident answer often influences everyone who speaks after it.

*How to reduce it:* ask participants to write down their thoughts individually before the group discussion starts.

### Social desirability bias

**Social desirability bias** happens when participants say what they think you want to hear. If they notice that you are excited about a feature, through your voice, face, or body language, they may hide their negative opinions.

*How to reduce it:* stay neutral, and tell participants that honest criticism helps you and won't hurt anyone's feelings.

### Availability bias

**Availability bias** in recruiting happens when you choose participants because they are easy to reach, not because they match your target users. This often happens under deadline pressure, for example when you skip screening questions to find participants faster.

*How to reduce it:* plan enough time for recruitment. If it's hard to find the right people, offer a better incentive, change your recruitment strategy, or ask for more time. Don't just take anyone who is available.

## Practical tips for unbiased research

1. **Choose your words carefully** - avoid leading questions that suggest an answer.
2. **Encourage independent thinking** - in groups, collect individual opinions before discussion.
3. **Avoid forcing answers** - in surveys, include options like "Other" or "I don't know", so people don't have to pick an answer that doesn't fit them.
4. **Let participants find their own way** - in usability studies, don't interrupt or guide them. Ask them to think aloud instead, so you understand their reasoning. Remember that people using assistive technology may follow a completely different path.
5. **Ask about mixed signals** - if someone's words and body language don't match, ask a gentle question like "How are you feeling about this so far?" instead of guessing.
6. **Make participants comfortable** - start with small talk and easy questions to reduce stress.
7. **Watch your own reactions** - keep your tone of voice and facial expressions neutral.
8. **Recruit the right people** - don't settle for participants who don't match your personas.
9. **Stay open-minded** - treat every answer equally, take notes, space out interviews, and invite a colleague to observe.
10. **Ask who is missing** - for every group you interview, think about who you are **not** talking to. For example, a team designing a food-ordering app may talk only to customers and forget the restaurant staff who receive the orders. Including all affected groups leads to fairer products.

::: tip Key takeaway
Everyone has biases. Learn their names, notice them in your own work, and use simple habits, like open-ended questions, detailed notes, neutral reactions, and careful recruitment, to keep your research honest.
:::
