# Paper wireframes for websites

## From sitemap to sketches

With the sitemap ready, you can start drawing pages. A **wireframe** is a simple outline of a screen, made mostly of lines, shapes, and a little text. It shows where things go on the page, not what they look like. You practiced paper wireframes for the app in [Chapter 3](../wireframes-and-prototypes/06-paper-wireframes); the process for a website is very similar, but pages are wider and there's usually more content to arrange.

Most designers start on paper because it's fast and cheap. You can draw a dozen ideas in the time it takes to build one digital screen, and you won't hesitate to throw away a sketch that doesn't work.

## Drawing conventions

Use the standard symbols, so other designers can read your sketches without explanation:

- **Images** - a rectangle with an X drawn through it.
- **Text** - horizontal lines; a thicker or shorter line can stand for a heading.
- **Buttons and calls to action** - rectangles or rounded shapes with a word inside, such as "Book".
- **Icons** - small circles or squares, labeled if the meaning isn't obvious.

A **call to action (CTA)** is an element that prompts the user to do something, such as "Sign up", "Add to cart", or "Next". Make CTAs easy to spot in your sketches, because they're often the most important elements on the page.

## How to sketch website wireframes

1. **List the content** - before drawing, write down every element the page needs, based on the sitemap and your user stories. For a homepage, that's typically a logo, navigation, a main message or image, the key content sections, and a footer.
2. **Draw the page outline** - draw a wide rectangle for a desktop screen. Draw a horizontal line where the fold will roughly be, so you remember what visitors see without scrolling.
3. **Work through the page** - many designers go from top to bottom, because that's how visitors experience a page, but you can start wherever you like. A typical website has three areas: the **header** at the top (logo, navigation, account or cart icons), the **body** with the main content, and the **footer** at the bottom.
4. **Put the most important content above the fold** - the main navigation and the main CTA usually belong there.
5. **Sketch at least five versions** - draw the same page in five different ways. Try something unusual at least once; ideas that seem silly sometimes lead somewhere good.
6. **Star the best parts** - compare the versions side by side and mark the strongest elements of each one with a star.
7. **Combine the stars** - draw a new wireframe that brings the starred elements together.
8. **Repeat for the other pages** - sketch, compare, and combine for every important page in the main user flow.

Keep your paper sketches and take photos of them. They'll guide your digital wireframes, and they show your process in your portfolio.

### The footer

The **footer** runs along the bottom of every page and holds secondary links that visitors may still need, such as contact details, social media links, privacy policy, terms of use, job openings, and an accessibility statement. Many websites show a full version of the logo there, with a smaller version in the header to save space.

### Header conventions

In left-to-right languages, people expect the logo at the top left and account and cart icons at the top right. Following such conventions means visitors don't have to search for the basics. In right-to-left languages such as Arabic and Hebrew, the layout is mirrored.

## Example: five WashSlot homepages

The element list for the WashSlot homepage includes the logo, the main navigation, an account icon, the status of the six machines, the resident's next booking, a "Book a slot" CTA, the latest notices, and a footer. The designer sketches five versions on a desktop-sized outline:

- **Version A** - a large status panel across the whole width with the six machines in one row, and the CTA directly below it.
- **Version B** - a left sidebar with the navigation, and the machines as a grid on the right.
- **Version C** - a short welcome message ("Good evening, Kofi") with the next booking, and the machine status further down.
- **Version D** - a timeline of the whole day, with each machine's bookings as colored bars, like a train timetable.
- **Version E** - just one big question, "Which machine is free?", with a single answer and a "Book it" button, and everything else below the fold.

The designer stars three things: the full-width row of machines from version A, because all six are visible at a glance; the "next booking" box from version C, because residents check it often; and the single, unmissable CTA from version E. The timeline from version D looks clever, but it's hard to read, especially in large text, so it doesn't get a star.

The combined wireframe has the header at the top, the row of six machine cards, then a two-column layer with "Your next booking" on the left and "Book a slot" on the right, a row of three notice cards, and the footer. The designer then repeats the process for the booking page, the summary, "My bookings", and the "House rules" page.

::: tip Key takeaway
Paper wireframes let you explore many layout ideas quickly. Use standard symbols, list the page content first, keep the main navigation and CTA above the fold, and sketch at least five versions of important pages. Star the best parts, combine them into one wireframe, and repeat for every page in the main user flow.
:::
