# Roles and responsibilities in UX

## Types of UX designers

"UX designer" is a broad title. In general, **UX designers focus on how people interact with a product**. In larger teams, this work is often split into more specific roles:

- **Interaction designers** - design how the product behaves and flows from step to step, always starting from the user's needs.
- **Visual designers** - design how the product looks, including layout, color, typography, and icons.
- **Motion designers** - design how it feels to move through the product, for example transitions between screens and small animations that give feedback.

## People who work with UX designers

UX designers rarely work alone. They collaborate with many other specialists:

- **Graphic designers** - create illustrations, logos, and marketing materials that communicate an idea through images.
- **UX researchers** - run studies and interviews to learn who the users are and how they use a product.
- **UX writers** - write the words inside the product, such as button labels, error messages, and onboarding screens, so that they are clear and helpful.
- **Production designers** - check that the first and final designs match and prepare design files so they are ready to hand off to engineers. They act as a bridge between design and engineering.
- **UX engineers** - turn the design into a working product with code, keeping the designer's intent intact.
- **UX program managers** - keep communication clear and on time so the project moves smoothly from start to finish.

### What are assets?

During a project, designers produce and share many **assets**. Assets are all the building blocks of a design: texts, images, icons, and the design specifications that describe them, such as font style, color, size, and spacing.

## Generalists, specialists, and T-shaped designers

As your career grows, you will probably fit into one of three profiles:

1. **Generalist** - a designer with a wide range of responsibilities who does a bit of everything: some interaction design, some visual design, some research. Generalists are common in small companies and startups.
2. **Specialist** - a designer who goes deep into one area, for example motion design, and builds deep expertise there. Specialists are more common in large companies with big design teams.
3. **T-shaped designer** - a designer who is an expert in one area (the vertical bar of the "T") and also has broad, basic knowledge of related areas (the horizontal bar). For example, a visual designer who also understands prototyping, research, writing, and a little code.

There is no "best" profile. Many designers start as generalists and specialize later.

## Where UX designers work

UX designers work in many kinds of places:

- **Large companies** - in-house design teams that work on the company's own products.
- **Startups** - new businesses developing a unique product; designers often wear many hats here.
- **Design agencies** - companies that design the look and experience of brands, products, and services for their clients.
- **Advertising agencies** - creative teams that build marketing campaigns for clients.
- **Freelancing** - designers who work for themselves and find their own clients.

Beginners often get their first experience through an **internship** (a temporary position where you learn while taking on smaller tasks) or an **apprenticeship** (on-the-job training that helps you build real skills).

## Responsibilities of an entry-level UX designer

When you start your first job, you will probably do a mix of the following tasks:

1. **User research** - learning about the people who use the product: their backgrounds, motivations, goals, emotions, and pain points. Typical methods are surveys, interviews, and observation.
2. **Information architecture (IA)** - deciding how the content of a product is organized and structured. IA works like the skeleton that holds a product together. For example, when you open the "File" menu in a text editor, you expect to see options like "New" and "Print", because that's where they are logically grouped.
3. **Wireframing** - drawing simple outlines of screens, mostly with lines, boxes, and a little text. Wireframes show where each element goes and let teammates give feedback early.
4. **Prototyping** - building an early model of the product that shows how it works. Prototypes can be paper or digital, simple or very detailed.
5. **Visual design** - understanding the basics of how a product looks, so you can explain how appearance and functionality support each other.
6. **Communication** - talking with your team through meetings, presentations, emails, and design tools. UX is very collaborative, so listening, accepting feedback, and explaining your ideas clearly are core skills.

Wireframes and prototypes have the same goal: **find the best design quickly and cheaply**, before engineers spend time building it.

## Working on a product

Most of the time you will either design something new (a new product or feature) or improve something that already exists. In both cases, start by **identifying the problems**: What isn't working? Why does the team want this feature at all? Once you understand the problems, the right solution, and the reasons behind it, become much clearer.

## Example: a cross-functional team

A **cross-functional team** brings together people with different skills to work toward one goal. Imagine a team building an app that helps drivers find and pay for parking:

- **Product lead** - sets the boundaries of the project and chooses the features for the first release.
- **UX researcher** - interviews drivers to find out what frustrates them about parking today.
- **Interaction designer** - plans the flow from searching for a spot to paying for it.
- **Visual designer** - makes sure free spots and prices are easy to read at a glance, even in bright sunlight.
- **Engineer** - turns wireframes and prototypes into a working app.
- **Program manager** - keeps everyone informed and the project on schedule.

## Resources to keep learning

Many organizations share free or paid materials about UX. These are good places to start:

- **[Nielsen Norman Group](https://www.nngroup.com/)** - research-based articles, reports, and training from one of the oldest UX consultancies.
- **[Interaction Design Foundation](https://www.interaction-design.org/)** - open articles, a large UX encyclopedia, and paid courses.
- **[UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/)** - a blog with articles written by designers from around the world.
- **[UX Planet](https://uxplanet.org/)** - articles for beginners and experienced designers, including career advice.
- **[Growth.design](https://growth.design/)** - case studies about product psychology told in a comic-book style.
- **[Case Study Club](https://www.casestudy.club/)** - a collection of UX case studies to learn from and get inspired by.
- **[Awwwards](https://www.awwwards.com/)** - a showcase of award-winning web design for visual inspiration.
- **[UX Podcast](https://uxpodcast.com/)** - a podcast about balancing business, technology, and people in digital work.

::: tip Key takeaway
UX is a team sport. Designers, researchers, writers, and engineers all shape the experience. As a beginner, expect to do a bit of everything: research, information architecture, wireframes, prototypes, visual design, and lots of communication.
:::
