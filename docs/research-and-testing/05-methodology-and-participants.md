# Methodology and participants

## Methodology

The **methodology** describes the steps you'll take to conduct the research, collect data, and analyze it. It answers practical questions: What method will you use? When and where will the sessions happen? Who will run them, and how?

A clear methodology has three benefits:

1. **It informs stakeholders** - they know what will happen, how long it will take, and where.
2. **It builds trust** - when stakeholders can see every step, they have more confidence in the results and are more likely to act on them.
3. **It makes the study repeatable** - other researchers can run the same study later and check whether they get similar results. That's what makes research reliable.

### Choosing the method

When choosing a method, think about the type of research it represents (remember [primary vs. secondary and qualitative vs. quantitative research](../foundations/12-ux-research)), the steps it involves, and the data it will produce.

At this stage of a project, with a low-fidelity prototype in hand, the most useful method is a **usability study**. It's primary research, because you collect the data yourself, and mostly qualitative, because you observe what participants do and say. You can watch participants directly during the session and also record it to review later.

### What to include in the methodology

- **Type of study** - for example, a moderated or unmoderated usability study.
- **Location** - in person (where?) or remote.
- **Dates and duration** - when the sessions happen and how long each one lasts.
- **Procedure** - what happens in each session, for example an introduction, a set of tasks, and a short questionnaire.
- **People** - who will moderate and who will take notes.

## Participants

Participants represent the voice of your real users, so choose them carefully. The participants section of the plan lists the **key characteristics** of the people you'll recruit, with a good reason for each one. Base these characteristics on your research goals and your personas.

### Avoid sample bias

**Sample bias** happens when participants come from a narrow group that doesn't represent all users, which skews the results. Imagine the WashSlot team recruiting only through a notice in the building's online chat group. Only residents who use that group, mostly younger and tech-savvy people, would see it, and the study would miss older residents and people without smartphones. The results would make the app seem easier to use than it really is.

Aim for a **representative sample**: a small group that reflects your key user groups **and** groups that are often marginalized. Marginalized users are often left out of research, so their needs are often missing from designs. Including them leads to better insights for everyone.

### How many participants?

For a usability study, **five to eight participants** are usually enough. Research shows that about five people uncover most major usability problems, and adding many more brings diminishing returns compared to the extra time and money.

### Screener surveys

A **screener survey** is a short questionnaire that checks whether potential participants have the characteristics you need. Ask only what's necessary, make sensitive questions optional, and use respectful wording.

### Where to find participants

- **Existing users** - if the product already has users, the company can invite some of them.
- **Online** - social media, design communities, recruiting agencies, and dedicated platforms that connect researchers with participants. Be aware that online recruiting can miss people who rarely use the internet.
- **Hallway testing** - asking people in a place where your target users are likely to be, such as a café, park, or office lobby. It's quick and cheap, but the people you meet may not match your target users.

### Incentives

An **incentive** is a reward that motivates people to take part and thanks them for their time, for example money, a gift card, a small gift, or a meal. Plan the incentive in advance and mention it in your plan.

## Including participants with disabilities

Learning how disabled people actually use your product is an essential part of research. Don't assume you know the workarounds they use; let them show you.

### Recruit by assistive technology, not by disability

Asking directly about someone's disability reduces them to one aspect of their identity, and disability status is sensitive personal information that, in many places, you're not allowed to ask about. Instead, **look for people who rely on the assistive technologies you plan to test**, for example:

- **Screen readers** - software that reads screen content aloud.
- **Captions** - text versions of audio content.
- **Switch devices** - buttons or other devices that replace a keyboard and mouse.
- **Keyboard-only navigation** - using a product without a mouse or touch.
- **Screen magnification** - tools that enlarge part or all of the screen.

### Accessibility is for everyone

Accessibility is sometimes shortened to **a11y**, because there are 11 letters between the "a" and the "y". The abbreviation also looks like the word "ally", a reminder that designers should be allies to people of all abilities.

Remember that disabilities can be **permanent** (for example, blindness), **temporary** (blurred vision after an eye operation), or **situational** (being unable to look at a screen while cycling). Assistive technologies range from screen readers, switches, and captions to reminder apps with simple images and **augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)** devices, which help people communicate with pictures instead of words.

### Try it yourself

Before the study, test the assistive technologies yourself. Turn on the screen reader on your phone and try a few of your favorite apps, and then your own prototype. You'll understand participants' experiences better and ask better questions.

## Example: WashSlot methodology and participants

### Methodology

- **Type** - moderated usability study.
- **Location** - four sessions in person in the building's common room, two sessions remote by video call.
- **Dates** - two afternoons and one evening, so residents with different schedules can join.
- **Duration** - 30 minutes per session: introduction, five tasks, and a short SUS questionnaire.
- **People** - the designer moderates; the developer takes notes.

### Participants

- **Six residents** aged 20 to 80, of different genders.
- **Different laundry habits** - some do laundry daily, others weekly.
- **Assistive technology** - at least one screen reader user and one person who uses large text.
- **Language** - at least one resident who doesn't speak the local language fluently.
- **Devices** - at least one resident who doesn't own a smartphone and tests on a tablet.
- **Incentive** - three free washing cycles, plus a thank-you note.

::: tip Key takeaway
The methodology explains how the study will run, so stakeholders trust it and others can repeat it. Choose five to eight participants who form a representative sample, avoid sample bias, use a screener survey, plan incentives, and recruit people who use assistive technologies rather than asking about disabilities.
:::
