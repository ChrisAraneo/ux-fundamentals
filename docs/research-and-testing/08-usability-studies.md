# Usability studies

## What is a usability study?

A **usability study** (also called **usability testing**) is a research method that checks how easily participants can complete the most important tasks in a design. Researchers watch participants as they use the product, and the feedback helps the team improve the experience. It's relatively cheap and low-risk, and it almost always reveals something valuable. Even negative feedback makes the product better.

## When to run a usability study

Usability studies can happen at many points in the design process:

- **Early ideas** - with a sketch or a low-fidelity prototype that is only partly interactive. This is often called **concept testing**.
- **Interactive prototypes** - the most common moment, when the team can still change the design before launch.
- **Finished products** - for example, before changing an existing feature or to check whether a product works for a specific group of people.

How many studies you run depends on the project's timeline, goals, and budget.

## An example

The WashSlot designer asks a participant to book a machine for Thursday evening. The participant finds the list of machines without trouble and taps machine 2. But on the booking screen, only today's times are shown. The participant scrolls up and down, then says: "I can't see Thursday anywhere. Can I only book for today?" The date selector was hidden behind a small calendar icon in the top bar that nobody noticed.

Without the study, the team might have launched an app in which booking for another day was nearly impossible to find.

## Moderated vs. unmoderated studies

### Moderated usability studies

In a **moderated** study, a person called the **moderator** guides participants through the session in real time, in person or online. The moderator gives tasks, asks follow-up questions, and helps when something is unclear.

#### Advantages

- **Build rapport** - a friendly relationship helps participants open up, which matters especially for sensitive topics.
- **Observe first-hand** - seeing reactions live has more impact than watching a recording. Stakeholders can observe too.
- **Guide participants** - the moderator makes sure participants complete exactly the tasks you want feedback on.
- **Explain complex flows** - good for testing complicated or unclear parts of a design, such as early low-fidelity prototypes.
- **Keep focus** - the moderator keeps the session on track.
- **Adjust language** - the moderator can put a confusing question in other words.
- **Ask for clarification** - the moderator can say "Tell me more about that", which leads to richer insights.

#### Disadvantages

- **Influence and bias** - the moderator's words, tone, or body language can affect answers.
- **Lack of connection** - participants who don't feel comfortable with the moderator may hold back.
- **Limited availability** - sessions usually happen during working hours, so people like shift workers or busy parents are harder to include.
- **Difficult scheduling** - two people's schedules must match, and no-shows are hard to reschedule.
- **Connection problems** - remote sessions need a reliable internet connection.
- **Higher cost** - a moderator's time and sometimes a room cost money.

### Unmoderated usability studies

In an **unmoderated** study, participants complete the tasks on their own, without a moderator. Their screen and voice are usually recorded, and the team reviews the recordings later.

#### Advantages

- **Natural behavior** - without supervision, people use the product more like they would in real life.
- **More participants** - you can include more people, across time zones and outside working hours.
- **Easier scheduling** - participants do the study whenever it suits them.
- **Lower cost** - no moderator and no room are needed.
- **More privacy** - people may share honest opinions more easily without someone watching them.
- **Good for simple flows** - straightforward tasks work well without guidance.

#### Disadvantages

- **No live support** - if a task is unclear or something breaks, nobody can help.
- **No follow-up questions** - you can't ask participants to explain what they meant.
- **Distractions** - you can't control the environment, so participants may multitask.
- **Risk of leaks** - you may have to upload unreleased designs to a testing platform, so you have less control over them.
- **Accessibility gaps** - some testing platforms don't work well with assistive technology.
- **More irrelevant data** - without a moderator, participants may drift off topic.
- **Only simple flows** - if someone gets stuck in a complex prototype, nobody can help them continue.

### Which one to choose?

Consider the study's goals and scope, the complexity of the flow, and the people you need to reach. *WashSlot example:* the designer chooses a **moderated** study, because the prototype is low-fidelity and some parts may be unclear, and because some participants are new to apps and may need reassurance.

## What stays the same

Whether a study is moderated or not, three things don't change:

- **Tasks** - realistic actions a real user would perform, such as booking a machine.
- **Script** - the same instructions and questions, in the same order, for every participant.
- **Participants** - people who represent your target users and match your personas.

::: tip Key takeaway
A usability study shows how easily people complete key tasks with your design. Moderated studies allow guidance and follow-up questions but cost more and risk influencing participants. Unmoderated studies are cheaper, more natural, and more flexible but offer no support or follow-up. Choose based on your goals, your prototype, and your participants.
:::
