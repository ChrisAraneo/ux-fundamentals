# From observations to insights

## What is synthesis?

After a usability study, you have a pile of notes, quotes, and recordings. The third step of a research study is to **analyze and synthesize** them. To **synthesize** means to combine ideas in order to draw conclusions. In UX, it means grouping observations into themes and turning those themes into **insights** that change how the team understands users and their needs.

After you have insights, you **iterate**: you revise the design to create a new and better version. Then you test again.

## Four steps from observations to insights

1. **Gather the data** - collect all notes, spreadsheets, sticky notes, and recordings from everyone who observed the study in one place.
2. **Organize the data** - arrange it so it's easier to understand, for example with an affinity diagram. If you used spreadsheet note-taking, you've already started.
3. **Find themes** - look for patterns that repeat across participants. Designers often use "patterns" and "themes" interchangeably; here, a pattern is what you see in the data, and a theme is what it means for the design.
4. **Write insights** - for each theme, write an insight that tells the team how to improve the product.

## Affinity diagrams

An **affinity** is a feeling of similarity or connection, like the bond you feel when you meet someone from your hometown abroad. An **affinity diagram** is a method that sorts observations into clusters that share a theme or a relationship. It's a fast, visual, and often collaborative way to make sense of many observations.

### How to create an affinity diagram

1. **Find a space** - a whiteboard, a window, an empty wall, or a digital whiteboard tool such as FigJam, Miro, or Mural.
2. **Write sticky notes** - put each observation or quote on its own note. If you took notes in a spreadsheet, copy them onto notes. Each note should contain **one idea in one sentence** that makes sense on its own. "Hard to read" is too vague; "Hard to read machine status on home screen" is clear. Put direct quotes in quotation marks.
3. **Mark participants** - give each participant their own sticky note color or number. This makes it easy to see how many people said similar things.
4. **Group the notes** - compare notes one by one and ask: "Is this similar to anything I've already placed?" If yes, put it in that group; if not, start a new one. You can start with groups you expect, or let groups emerge as you go.
5. **Continue until all notes are placed** - aim for roughly three to ten groups. A few notes may not fit anywhere. That's normal, but consider them before setting them aside, because a single participant can still point to a real problem.
6. **Review again** - take a break, then look at the groups with fresh eyes. Move notes, split large groups into subgroups, or create new groups. There are no "right" answers.

Affinity diagrams can be adapted for participants or teammates with visual impairments: someone can dictate what to write on each note, and the notes and groups can be read aloud so everyone can suggest groupings.

### WashSlot example

The six WashSlot sessions produced about 60 sticky notes. The team grouped them like this:

- **Choosing a day** - "Only today's times visible" (P1, P2, P4, P5, P6), "Didn't notice calendar icon" (P2, P3, P5), "Why can I only see today?" (P2).
- **Machine status** - "Wanted to know how long until a busy machine is free" (P1, P3, P4, P6), "Status icons not read by screen reader" (P3).
- **Reminders** - "Didn't see the reminder switch" (P2, P4, P6), "Expected a reminder automatically" (P4).
- **Readability** - "Text too small on status labels" (P1, P5).
- **House rules** - "Looked for rules in the menu, not settings" (P3, P5).

## From patterns to themes

To find themes, ask two questions:

1. **Which patterns appear again and again in the data?**
2. **What do they tell us about the design?**

Groups with many notes from different participants usually show patterns. With six participants, groups that include three or more people are a good place to start. A simple template turns each pattern into a theme:

> It was observed that **[number] out of [total] participants [pattern]**. This means that **[what it tells us about the design]**.

*WashSlot examples:*

1. It was observed that **5 out of 6 participants** couldn't find how to book for another day. This means that **the way to change the day is almost invisible**.
2. It was observed that **4 out of 6 participants** wanted to know when a busy machine would be free. This means that **"busy" alone isn't enough information to plan laundry**.
3. It was observed that **3 out of 6 participants** didn't notice the reminder switch. This means that **the reminder option is easy to miss on the summary screen**.

Keep themes simple: one idea per theme, plain language, and objective observations, not opinions.

## Writing strong insights

An insight turns a theme into direction for the team. Strong insights are:

1. **Grounded in real data** - based on what you observed, supported by several participants, not on what you felt.
2. **Connected to the research questions** - they help answer the questions from your research plan, so people understand why they matter.
3. **Easy to understand** - anyone, even someone who didn't see the study, should understand them.
4. **Empathy-building** - they help the team feel what users felt, which motivates them to fix the problem.
5. **Actionable** - they point to what should change. "The app is useful" isn't actionable; "Residents need to see and change the day directly on the booking screen" is.

*WashSlot insights:*

- **Day selection** - residents need to see and change the day directly on the booking screen.
- **Machine status** - residents need to know how much time is left on a busy machine.
- **Reminders** - residents expect a reminder by default and need a clearly visible way to turn it off.
- **Accessibility** - status labels need larger text and labels that screen readers can read.

## Fall in love with the problem

When participants suggest solutions, listen, but don't adopt them blindly. One participant's solution may work for them but not for everyone else. Focus on understanding the problem and **why** it happened. Then you can design a solution that works for many people and fits the whole experience.

::: tip Key takeaway
Synthesis turns observations into insights in four steps: gather the data, organize it (for example in an affinity diagram), find themes, and write insights. Strong insights are based on data, answer your research questions, are easy to understand, build empathy, and inspire action.
:::
