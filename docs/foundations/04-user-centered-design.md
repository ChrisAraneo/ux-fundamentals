# User-centered and inclusive design

## Who is a user?

A **user** is any person who uses a product. Most often, a user is someone with a problem who turns to a product or service in the hope of solving it. The **user experience** is the whole journey that person goes through with the product, from first contact to the moment the problem is solved.

## User-centered design

**In user-centered design, every decision starts with the user.** Instead of starting with "What can we build?", the team starts with "Who are we building for, and what do they need?" It means taking the user's situation, feelings, and the insights gathered about them seriously.

### The user-centered design process

The process usually has four steps:

1. **Understand** - learn how users experience the product today and in what context they use it.
2. **Specify** - identify the users' needs and decide which problem is the most important to solve.
3. **Design** - create a solution to that problem.
4. **Evaluate** - check the solution against the users' needs, ideally with real users.

### Iteration is key

These steps are not done only once. **Iteration** means repeating a process and building on the previous version each time, with small improvements. After the evaluate step, you usually go back to understanding or designing, and the design gets better with every round.

## Getting to know your users

A common mistake is to have a narrow picture of the user or to rely on stereotypes. People who look very different on the surface may share the same needs, and people who look similar may need very different things. Take a step back and think about **all** your potential users, especially those who are often overlooked.

### Questions to ask about your users

Use these questions to widen your view:

- **Do my users have any disabilities?** Think about permanent, temporary, and situational ones. A person with one arm, a person with a broken arm, and a parent holding a baby all need a product that works with one hand.
- **How familiar are my users with technology?** This is called **digital literacy**. Don't design only for current users who are already comfortable with the product; think also about people who are just starting out.
- **How do my users access the product?** For example, on an old phone with a slow connection, on a shared family computer, or with a screen reader.
- **Where and when do my users use the product?** A product used in many countries must respect different languages and cultural norms. A product used outdoors must be readable in bright light.
- **Have I really considered all my potential users?** If the answer is "probably not", go back and look for the groups you missed.

## Accessibility

**Accessibility** means designing so that people with disabilities can use a product, device, service, or place. Accessible features often help everyone. Captions on videos help deaf people, but also anyone watching on a noisy train. Larger fonts help people with low vision, but also anyone reading on a small screen.

You will learn more about the tools people with disabilities use on the [assistive technology](./05-assistive-technology) page.

## Universal, inclusive, and equity-focused design

These three approaches all try to make products work for more people, but each one goes about it differently.

### Universal design

**Universal design** means creating **one product** that can be used by people with the widest possible range of abilities, in the widest possible range of situations. You can think of it as a "one size fits all" solution. A classic example is an automatic door: it works for wheelchair users, parents with strollers, delivery workers carrying boxes, and everyone else.

### Inclusive design

**Inclusive design** means making design choices that consider personal characteristics such as ability, ethnicity, income, language, age, and gender. While universal design looks for one solution for everyone, inclusive design is often described as **"solve for one, extend to many"**: you solve a problem for one specific group, and the solution turns out to benefit many others.

Example: keyboard shortcuts were important for people who can't use a mouse, but today many power users rely on them to work faster.

### Equity-focused design

**Equity-focused design** goes one step further than inclusive design. It pays special attention to **groups that product teams have overlooked or left out in the past**, and it aims to make the product fair for them. It means checking every part of a product to make sure it is accessible and fair for people of all genders, ethnicities, and abilities.

Forms are a good place to practice this. Registration forms, job applications, and official surveys often ask for sensitive information such as gender or ethnicity. Offering options beyond a strict binary choice and allowing people to skip questions they don't want to answer are a starting point, not the finish line.

## Equality vs. equity

These two terms sound similar, but they are different:

- **Equality** - everyone gets the same amount of support or the same resources.
- **Equity** - each person gets the support they need, so that everyone can reach a fair outcome.

Imagine three people of different heights trying to look over a fence. Equality gives each of them one box of the same size, so the shortest person still can't see. Equity gives each person as many boxes as they need, so all three can see the game.

::: tip Key takeaway
Aim for a product that feels made for each person who uses it, whatever their background, location, income, abilities, or education. Start by getting to know your users broadly and without stereotypes.
:::
