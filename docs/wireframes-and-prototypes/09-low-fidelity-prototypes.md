# Low-fidelity prototypes

## From wireframe to prototype

UX work moves in loops of steady improvement: you create something, get feedback, and improve it. The next step in that cycle is to turn your wireframes into a **low-fidelity prototype**: a simple, interactive model that gives a basic idea of how the product will look and work. Its goal is to make your design **testable** early, so you can collect feedback before investing more time.

What separates a low-fidelity prototype from a wireframe is **interactivity**: the ability to move from one screen to another.

- **In a paper prototype** - a person plays the role of the computer and switches paper screens by hand when the user "taps" something.
- **In a digital prototype** - wireframes are connected, so clicking an element, such as a "Submit" button, opens the next screen.

## Paper prototypes

### Benefits

Paper prototypes have many advantages:

- **Inexpensive** - all you need is paper, a pen, and scissors.
- **Quick to change** - you can create many versions and fix mistakes in seconds.
- **Low commitment** - throwing away a paper prototype doesn't hurt, unlike a digital prototype that took hours to build.
- **Honest feedback** - because they look unfinished, people feel more comfortable criticizing them.
- **Collaborative** - everyone on the team can sketch their own screens and combine them.

### Drawbacks

Paper prototypes also have limits:

- **They require imagination** - testers have to picture what the real product will be like.
- **They work only in person** - someone has to be in the room to switch the screens.
- **They're hard for remote teams** - building and testing them is hands-on work that's best done together.

### When to use paper prototypes

Paper prototypes are great during brainstorming sessions, when you want to compare several ideas quickly, and for testing an early product idea with users. Remember that the path isn't fixed: some projects go straight from paper wireframes to digital prototypes, and some well-established products skip paper entirely. Choose what works for your project and team.

## How to build a paper prototype

### 1. Prepare the screens

Draw (or reuse) a wireframe for every screen in your user flow, and cut each one out. You also need a **phone outline**, a piece of cardboard or paper shaped like a phone frame, that you place over the screens.

### 2. Arrange the screens in order

Lay the screens out in the order a user will experience them, from left to right. Screens that belong to other flows, such as the profile or settings, can be placed above, below, or to the side.

*WashSlot example:* the designer arranges Home, Booking, Summary, and Confirmation from left to right, and puts "My bookings" and "Settings" in a separate row below.

### 3. Walk through a scenario

Give the tester a concrete task, then act as the computer. When the tester "taps" a button on the paper screen, replace the screen with the right next one.

*WashSlot example:* "Book machine 2 for Thursday at 19:00." The tester taps "Book a slot", chooses the machine and time, turns on the reminder, and taps "Confirm".

### 4. Fix what's missing

You will almost certainly discover gaps. When the WashSlot tester tapped "Confirm", the designer realized there was no screen for the case where the slot was booked by someone else in the meantime. With paper, you can draw the missing screen in a minute and continue the test. Finding these gaps early is exactly why you build prototypes.

### Simulate scrolling

For a long screen, such as a long list of time slots, draw it on a tall strip of paper. Place the phone outline over the top part and slowly slide the strip upward to simulate scrolling.

## Why design for mobile first?

Many teams start with the mobile version, because the phone is the smallest screen the design will appear on. If the design works within those limits, it will usually adapt well to larger screens. Still, choose the starting platform based on what is best for your users and the business.

## Tools that bridge paper and digital

Some apps let you photograph your paper sketches and add clickable areas to them, turning the photos into a simple digital prototype you can try on a phone. They are a quick middle step between paper and a full design tool.

::: tip Key takeaway
A low-fidelity prototype is a wireframe that users can interact with. Paper prototypes are cheap, fast, and invite honest feedback, but they need imagination and an in-person tester. Cut out your screens, arrange them in order, walk testers through a task, and fix the gaps you discover.
:::
