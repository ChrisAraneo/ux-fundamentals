# UX jobs and resumes

## Job titles to search for

With two projects in your portfolio, you can start looking at real job openings. Job titles in UX vary a lot between companies, so it helps to know what each one usually means. You met the main roles in [Chapter 1](../foundations/02-ux-roles); here is what to look for in job postings.

Most designers start as **generalists**, with a broad range of tasks. Some later become **specialists** who go deep into one area. Common titles include:

| Title | Focus | Words to look for in postings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **UX designer** | A generalist role covering anything from structure to visuals to interaction | User research, wireframes, prototypes, usability testing |
| **Interaction designer** | How a product works, connecting user needs and business goals with what can actually be built | User flows, wireframes, mockups, prototypes |
| **Visual designer** | How a product looks | Typography, color, iconography, layout, branding |
| **Motion designer** | How it feels to move through a product | Transitions, animation, triggers, micro-interactions |
| **UI designer** | The look and function of the interface, with less focus on research or information architecture | Interface design, components, design systems |
| **Product designer** | How the whole product comes together, often working closely with branding and marketing | End-to-end design, visual and interaction design, product strategy |

These important words in a job posting are called **key terms**. They tell you what the job really involves, so read the description carefully instead of judging by the title alone. Some companies use "UI designer" and "UX designer" for the same job.

For your first role, cast a wide net. It's hard to know which specialty suits you before you have real experience. Generalist roles are a great way to learn many skills, and you can specialize later. A hint from your own work: if you enjoyed wireframes and flows most, look at interaction design; if you loved polishing mockups, visual design; if prototyping animations was your favorite part, motion design.

## Ways to start your career

1. **Internship** - a temporary position with a narrow set of tasks, done under the close guidance of a supervisor. It gives you real experience and sometimes leads to a full-time job.
2. **Apprenticeship** - paid, on-the-job training alongside an experienced professional. It usually lasts longer than an internship, often one to two years.
3. **Freelancing** - working for yourself and finding your own clients. Offering your services to a small business or a nonprofit is a good way to start: you gain a real project for your portfolio and a recommendation.
4. **Entry-level job** - a role that doesn't require previous experience in the field. This is the most common way into UX, and the portfolio you've built in this course is exactly what entry-level hiring managers want to see.

## Types of employers

| Employer | What it's like for a designer |
| --- | --- |
| **Startup** | A new business building a unique product. Budgets are tight and teams are small, so you'll be a generalist, move fast, and take on a lot of responsibility. It can be stressful but teaches you quickly. |
| **Advertising agency** | A team of creatives that builds marketing campaigns for clients. You work on many different brands, but as an employee in a team rather than alone. |
| **Design agency or studio** | A company that designs the look and experience of brands, products, and services for clients. You'll often join projects partway through, move between teams, and see many industries. |
| **Corporation** | A large company with thousands of employees and many projects. Designers are usually specialists, follow one project from start to finish, and learn from many experts. |

As you browse openings, also narrow your search by location, remote options, and the experience level required. Note the postings that interest you and the key terms that repeat; they show which skills to highlight.

## Designing your resume

You already know what every resume needs: your contact details, education, and work history. As a UX designer, your resume is also a small design project. Approach it the way you'd approach any design: think about the reader, apply hierarchy, grids, color, and negative space, and express your **personal brand**.

Many designers write the content in a document editor first, then refine the layout in a design tool such as Figma, just as wireframes come before mockups. Common design choices include:

- **Two columns** - a two-column grid lines elements up neatly and makes the resume faster to scan than a single block of text.
- **One accent color** - a single color for your name and section headings adds personality. Choose a readable color (pale yellow on white won't work) and use the same color as on your portfolio website.
- **A personal logo** - a simple mark made from your initials, used on your resume, portfolio, and business cards, ties your brand together.
- **Bullet points** - short bullets are easier to skim than paragraphs, and the bullet symbol itself can carry your accent color.
- **Intentional typography** - for example, one typeface for your name and another for the text, with former employers in bold and dates in a lighter weight, so recruiters see the hierarchy at a glance.

### What to write

- **Link to your portfolio** - recruiters want to see your work, so make the link easy to find.
- **Show your own contribution** - say clearly what **you** did on team projects, and describe the impact, not just a list of duties.
- **Use your earlier experience** - even unrelated jobs show valuable skills, such as leadership, teamwork, working under pressure, or problem solving. Long tenure at one employer shows reliability.
- **Include design work outside jobs** - volunteer projects, course projects, certificates, and design exercises all show your process and craft.

### Final checks

Save the file in the format the job posting asks for, such as PDF or Word. Check spelling and grammar carefully, and ask someone to review the resume whenever you make big changes. A career counselor or an employment office can also give you a professional opinion.

## Preparing to talk about your work

In interviews, you'll be asked to explain how you work. Prepare a clear, step-by-step description of your **problem-solving process** (for example: ask clarifying questions, identify constraints and dependencies, explore options) and of your **design process**. Then practice telling the story of your favorite projects from start to finish, focusing on the decisions you made and the results. Rejections are part of every designer's career, even for the best designers. Keep improving your work and keep applying.

*WashSlot example:* the WashSlot designer previously worked for five years as a dispatcher at a delivery company. Their new resume uses two columns: skills, tools, and contact details on the left; projects, experience, and education on the right. The accent color is the deep teal from WashSlot, which also appears on their portfolio website. The dispatcher job isn't hidden; it's described through what it taught them: "Coordinated 40 drivers' daily routes and resolved schedule conflicts under time pressure." That experience is surprisingly close to designing a booking system for shared machines, and it gives the designer a good story for interviews.

::: tip Key takeaway
UX job titles vary, so read the key terms in each posting instead of judging by the title. Common ways in are internships, apprenticeships, freelancing, and entry-level jobs, at startups, agencies, or corporations. Design your resume like any other product: clear hierarchy, a two-column grid, a consistent personal brand, a link to your portfolio, and a focus on your own impact.
:::
