# Empathy in UX

## What is empathy?

**Empathy** is the ability to understand another person's thoughts and feelings in a specific situation, as if you were in their place. In UX design, empathy is the starting point of everything: it helps you experience a product the way your users do, so you can design something that truly fits their lives.

Like everyone else, designers make assumptions. Yet the job is to solve the problems users **really** face, not the ones **we imagine** they face. Empathy, supported by research, helps you replace guesses with real understanding.

## Pity, sympathy, and empathy

These three words are often confused, but they describe very different attitudes:

- **Pity** - feeling sorry for someone, often from a position of superiority. It creates distance rather than understanding.
- **Sympathy** - recognizing someone's feelings and showing concern, but without sharing those feelings yourself.
- **Empathy** - trying to understand and feel what the other person experiences, from their point of view.

Imagine a user who says, "I always miss the bus because the timetable app is so slow." Sympathy sounds like "That's unfortunate." Empathy sounds like "It must be stressful to stand at the stop, refresh the app, and still not know whether to run." Only the second response helps you design a better app.

## How to build empathy

Empathy is a skill, and you can practice it. These habits help:

- **Ask many questions** - don't assume what users need. Ask them, and use questions that start with "what", "how", and "why" to understand their point of view.
- **Observe carefully** - pay attention to the whole person, not only their words. Facial expressions, hesitation, and body language often tell you more than answers do.
- **Listen actively** - focus completely on what the person is saying, instead of planning your next question or waiting for your turn to talk.
- **Ask a variety of people for input** - friends and colleagues tend to give friendly, positive feedback. Look for honest opinions from a diverse group of real users.
- **Keep an open mind** - everyone has biases. Notice yours and set them aside so they don't color what users tell you.
- **Stay curious about research** - follow UX researchers and communities to keep learning how people think and behave.

## Empathy before research: empathy exercises

Good designers start empathizing even before they talk to users. In an **empathy exercise**, you imagine a specific person and try to predict how they would experience a situation. This helps you prepare better research questions.

For practice, designers sometimes use **user profiles**: short, fictional biographies of possible users. Don't confuse them with personas. A user profile is an informal tool for imagining users before research. A **persona** is created after research and represents a real group of users (you will learn about personas [later in this chapter](./06-personas)).

### Example: an empathy exercise for WashSlot

Before interviewing residents, the WashSlot designer imagines a profile: *a 23-year-old student who works evening shifts at a restaurant, comes home after 22:00, and is still learning the local language.*

The designer then asks themself the planned interview questions and imagines the answers:

- **"How do you organize your laundry?"** - probably late at night, when the laundry room is empty, but maybe the building has quiet hours.
- **"What is difficult about using the laundry room?"** - the paper sign-up sheet and the house rules might be written only in the local language.

The exercise reveals two things. First, the designer should ask about quiet hours and house rules. Second, the word "difficult" might make people feel they are expected to complain, so the designer rewrites the question in a more neutral way: "Tell me about the last time you did laundry here."

Empathy exercises never replace real research. They help you go into research better prepared.

::: tip Key takeaway
Empathy means understanding users from their own point of view, not feeling sorry for them. Practice it by asking, observing, listening, and keeping an open mind, and start even before your research begins.
:::
