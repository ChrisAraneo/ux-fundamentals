# Sharing insights

## Two formats for sharing insights

The fourth step of a research study is to share and promote your insights, so stakeholders understand them and act on them. Two formats are common:

- **Presentation** - a set of slides (often called a **deck**), each with new information. It gives a high-level overview, usually focusing on the three to five most important insights, and works well for large groups and people outside UX. Tools include Google Slides, PowerPoint, and Keynote.
- **Research report** - a document with the same information but fewer visuals. Reports often put the summary of insights and recommendations first, so busy readers get the key points immediately. Tools include Google Docs, Word, and Pages.

## Structure of a research presentation

A typical research presentation has 15 to 20 slides:

1. **Title slide** - a simple title, the date, and the names of the team members.
2. **Table of contents** - the sections of the presentation, so the audience knows what's coming.
3. **Project background** - why the research was done and what decisions depend on it.
4. **Study details** - the research questions, participants (number and key characteristics), and methodology (format, length, location, procedure). Include clear numbers, which make the study credible.
5. **Prototype** - screenshots of the design that participants tested.
6. **Themes** - one slide per theme, with the theme as the heading, evidence as bullet points, a supporting quote from a participant, and a screenshot of the problem.
7. **Insights** - three to five insights, each with a short name and at most two sentences of explanation.
8. **Recommendations** - at least three concrete actions, ordered by importance. If an insight describes a problem, the recommendation suggests a solution.
9. **Thank you** - a closing slide.
10. **Appendix** - extra details, such as a full participant list, that you don't present but can show if someone asks.

Give each section its own title slide with large text and little else, so the audience always knows where they are.

### Make slides accessible

Add **alt text** to every image, so people using screen readers can understand the slides when they review them later. In most presentation tools, you can right-click an image and choose an option such as "Alt text" to add a description.

## Tips for a strong presentation

1. **Know your audience** - learn what they care about and adapt. A presentation for engineers differs from one for marketing or management.
2. **Start with an overview** - include a roadmap and an **executive summary**, a slide with your main conclusions up front. Show how the research connects to the organization's goals.
3. **Tell a story** - give the presentation a beginning, middle, and end, and include real users' voices through quotes, photos, or short video clips. Stories stay in memory much longer than lists.
4. **Show data** - charts and numbers often communicate more clearly than words.
5. **Use little text** - slides should support what you say, not replace it. Put details in the appendix.
6. **End with recommendations** - clear next steps help stakeholders support your proposals.
7. **Leave time for questions** - questions show what your audience cares about and let you clarify.

## Preparing to present

- **Write speaker notes** - add two or three key points per slide in the notes section, then turn each into one starting sentence. Don't write everything you'll say; reading a full script sounds robotic.
- **Practice aloud** - rehearse the whole presentation, out loud, without an audience. You'll notice awkward phrases and parts that take too long.
- **Describe images aloud** - when a slide shows a screen or chart, describe it, so everyone, including people with visual impairments, can follow.
- **Record yourself** - use a phone or a video-call tool to record a rehearsal, then watch it and note what to improve.

## Delivering the presentation

Great presenters aren't born that way; they practice. These habits help:

- **Be concise** - stick to the points the audience needs to remember.
- **Speak naturally** - talk as you would to a colleague, not like you're reading.
- **Use stories** - specific, relatable examples keep people engaged.
- **Pause** - pauses show confidence and give people time to think. Good moments for a pause are after a question and between sections.
- **Speak clearly and slowly** - nervous speakers tend to rush.
- **Make eye contact** - look at one person for a few seconds, then move to the next. If that feels uncomfortable, look at people's foreheads or just past them. On video calls, look into the camera.
- **Use confident body language** - stand naturally, move a little, smile, and avoid crossed arms or fidgeting. Online, use facial expressions and visible hand gestures.
- **Be yourself** - people connect with presenters who are genuine.

## Dealing with stage fright

Feeling nervous before speaking is completely normal, even for experienced speakers. These tips help:

- **Prepare** - know your content well and rehearse several times.
- **Think positively** - imagine the presentation going well instead of imagining everything that could go wrong.
- **Breathe** - slow, deep breaths calm your body.
- **Sleep well** - a rested mind concentrates better.
- **Remember the goal** - the audience wants information, not perfection.
- **Accept mistakes** - stumbling over a word or pausing to think is no problem. Most people in the room have been in your place.

## Handling questions and objections

- **Know your material** - the better you know your research, the easier it is to answer. Prepare for the questions you expect.
- **Use stories** - real users' experiences are strong arguments.
- **Repeat the question** - so everyone hears it, and to check you understood.
- **Don't get defensive** - objections aren't personal. Stay polite and keep any disagreement about ideas, not people.
- **Be brief** - give others a chance to ask too, and offer to follow up later if time runs out.
- **Take your time** - it's fine to say "That's a good question; let me think for a moment", or "I'd like to check that and get back to you."

## Example: WashSlot presentation

The WashSlot designer presents to the cooperative's board with 16 slides: background, study details (six residents aged 23 to 79, 30-minute moderated sessions), four theme slides with quotes such as *"Why can I only see today?"*, an insights slide, and three recommendations:

1. **Show the days** - display the next seven days as tabs at the top of the booking screen.
2. **Make status accessible** - make status labels larger and give every icon a text label that screen readers can read.
3. **Show remaining time** - show how many minutes are left on busy machines.

::: tip Key takeaway
Share insights in a presentation or a report. A strong presentation knows its audience, starts with a summary, tells a story with real users' voices, shows data, uses little text, and ends with clear recommendations. Prepare speaker notes, practice aloud, and handle questions calmly.
:::
