# User journey maps

## What is a user journey map?

A **user journey** covers every step and experience on a person's way to a particular goal. A **user journey map** is a visual representation of that journey, step by step, including what the user does and how they feel along the way.

Think of it like a book: the persona is the main character, the user story is the plot, and the journey map is the outline of the story.

At the beginning of a project, a journey map usually describes how the user reaches their goal **today**, before your product exists. It focuses on the user's whole day and situation, not only on screens.

## Why create a journey map?

User journey maps have several benefits:

- **They reveal obstacles** - like a navigation app that warns you about traffic, a journey map shows where users get stuck, so you can design a smoother path.
- **They reduce designer bias** - **designer bias** is the tendency to design for your own needs and preferences instead of the user's. Mapping each step from the persona's perspective keeps you focused on them, not on yourself.
- **They uncover new pain points** - problems you would never notice from your own experience become visible when you follow someone else's journey.
- **They show opportunities** - each difficult moment in the journey is a chance to improve the experience.

## How to create a user journey map

Work on one persona at a time, so details from different people don't get mixed up.

1. **Choose a persona and a goal** - write the persona's name and the goal they want to achieve at the top of the map.
2. **List the actions** - break the journey into the main actions the user takes, in a logical order from the first need to the completed goal.
3. **Add tasks to each action** - describe the smaller steps the user must complete within each action. Two or three tasks per action are usually enough.
4. **Describe feelings** - for every action, note at least one emotion the user is likely to feel, such as confused, stressed, relieved, or happy.
5. **Identify improvement opportunities** - for the difficult moments, write ideas that could remove the problem, and for the good moments, ideas that could make them even better. Look for missing needs, confusing steps, and duplicated or unnecessary steps.
6. **Consider accessibility** - check every step for users with different abilities. Could someone with limited vision, hearing, mobility, or speech complete it?
7. **Check for bias** - review the map and ask whether it reflects the persona's real experience or your own assumptions.

## Example: Grace's laundry journey

**Persona:** Grace, 74. **Goal:** wash and dry a week's laundry without stress.

| | Plan laundry | Go to the laundry room | Wash and dry | Collect laundry |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Tasks** | Check the calendar; remember the paper sheet; decide on a day | Carry the basket to the elevator; walk to the basement; check the sign-up sheet | Load the machine; pay with a card; wait upstairs | Return at the right time; unload; carry the basket up |
| **Feelings** | Uncertain | Tired, disappointed when all machines are busy | Worried she won't know when it's done | Embarrassed when a neighbor has already moved her clothes |
| **Opportunities** | Show free slots on her tablet in large text | Let her book a slot so a machine is guaranteed | Send a visual alert and a vibration when the cycle ends | Give a reminder 10 minutes before the cycle ends; allow a grace period before others can use the machine |

Following Grace's journey revealed a pain point that interviews with younger residents never mentioned: she cannot hear the machine's beep. Without the map, the team might have focused only on booking and missed the notification problem entirely.

## Check your journey map

When your map is finished, ask yourself whether you have:

- **Relevant actions** - covered the whole journey from the first need to the reached goal.
- **Detailed tasks** - listed two or three tasks for each action.
- **Feelings** - named at least one emotion for every action.
- **Opportunities** - found improvement ideas for at least three steps.
- **Accessibility** - considered users with different abilities at every step.
- **Less bias** - described the persona's experience, not your own.

::: tip Key takeaway
A user journey map follows one persona through the actions, tasks, and feelings involved in reaching a goal. It reduces designer bias, reveals hidden pain points, and turns every difficult moment into an opportunity for a better design.
:::
