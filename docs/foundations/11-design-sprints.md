# Design sprints

## What is a design sprint?

A **design sprint** is a time-boxed process, usually five full working days, in which a team solves one important design challenge by **designing, prototyping, and testing ideas with users**. Instead of discussing a problem for months, the team focuses on it intensively for one week.

## Why run a design sprint?

Design sprints have several benefits:

- **They save time** - decisions that would normally take months are made in a few days.
- **They put the user first** - the sprint ends with real users testing the solution.
- **They reduce risk** - the team learns whether an idea works before spending a lot of money building it.
- **They value everyone's input** - people from different departments contribute, and the best ideas win, no matter who suggested them.
- **They create focus** - for one week, the team works on a single challenge without distractions.
- **They are flexible** - the sprint format can be adjusted to the team's schedule and needs.

## When is a design sprint a good idea?

Before you plan a sprint, look at your design challenge and ask:

1. **Are there many possible solutions?** - sprints are great for exploring and choosing between options.
2. **Do people from several teams need to be involved?** - sprints bring different experts into one room.
3. **Is the challenge big enough?** - a tiny change, like fixing a typo, doesn't need a whole week.

If the answer to at least one of these questions is "yes", a design sprint may be the right choice.

## The five phases of a design sprint

Each phase usually takes one day:

1. **Understand** - the team explores the design challenge, listens to short talks by experts, and agrees on the goal.
2. **Ideate** - everyone generates as many solutions as possible, often using sketching techniques.
3. **Decide** - the team compares the ideas and votes for the one with the best chance of success. Planning for the test day also starts now.
4. **Prototype** - the team builds a realistic prototype that users can try.
5. **Test** - real users try the prototype, and the team observes and interviews them to learn what works.

## Design sprints vs. design thinking

Design sprints and the design thinking framework look similar. Both have five phases, put users first, and rely on collaboration, quick prototyping, and testing. But they are not the same:

| | Design thinking | Design sprint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Phases** | Empathize, define, ideate, prototype, test | Understand, ideate, decide, prototype, test |
| **What it is** | A general philosophy of problem solving | A specific plan for solving one specific challenge |
| **Duration** | Open-ended, spans the whole product development process | Strictly time-boxed, usually five working days |
| **Methods** | Uses many different methods and tools | One method with a fixed process |

Design sprints can be used **inside** design thinking to speed up a specific phase. For example, a sprint can be used to quickly gather user insights during empathize, to align stakeholders on a problem statement during define, or to generate many ideas during ideate. Sprints are strongest in the prototype and test phases, where they quickly turn ideas into something users can try.

## Planning a design sprint

Good sprints need careful preparation. If you organize one, follow these steps:

1. **Do user research** - research is always the first step. Focus it on the user problems the sprint will tackle. You don't need a detailed problem yet, but a rough idea helps you choose research methods.
2. **Invite experts** - schedule short talks with colleagues or industry experts who know the problem well.
3. **Find the right space** - choose a room with whiteboards and walls for sticky notes, good acoustics, comfortable movable furniture, and accommodations for anyone who needs them. For remote sprints, prepare a shared online whiteboard.
4. **Gather supplies** - make sure everyone has markers, sticky notes, paper, and plenty of water and snacks.
5. **Set the rules** - decide on rules in advance, for example "no laptops or phones except during breaks".
6. **Plan introductions** - sprint teams often include people who have never worked together, so plan a short icebreaker.
7. **Plan what happens after the sprint** - decide how the results will be used and ask a few people to document everything with photos, notes, and collected sticky notes.

## The sprint brief

A **sprint brief** is a document that you send to all participants before the sprint so they can prepare. It usually contains:

- **Sprint challenge** - the goal of the sprint in one or two sentences.
- **Key deliverables** - what the team should produce by the end, for example a tested prototype.
- **Logistics** - where and when the sprint takes place, who is attending, and who leads it.
- **Approvers** - people who must sign off on the result before it moves forward.
- **Resources** - the people, tools, and budget available; long sprints may need a plan to secure more.
- **Project overview** - the current status, known roadblocks, early wins, and the expected launch plan.
- **Sprint schedule** - an hour-by-hour plan for every day, including breaks.

## Your role as an entry-level designer

As a beginner, you won't lead the sprint, but you will contribute to every phase:

1. **Understand** - listen carefully to the expert talks and write down your thoughts and questions.
2. **Ideate** - share as many ideas as you can; this is your main task on this day.
3. **Decide** - take part in discussions and vote for the solution you believe in. You may also help prepare the test day by recruiting testers, writing interview questions, and gathering equipment.
4. **Prototype** - help build the prototype, ask questions, suggest improvements, and review the finished version.
5. **Test** - observe and interview users and collect their feedback, which shows the team what to change before launch.

A sprint is intense. At the end, you will probably feel tired but full of energy and new ideas.

## Retrospectives

A **retrospective** is a meeting where the team reviews the sprint itself and critiques how it went. It usually happens right after the sprint, while everyone still remembers the details. It doesn't need a strict agenda; the goal is to let **everyone** share their feedback. A retrospective answers two main questions: **What went well?** and **What can we improve next time?**

Speak up during retrospectives, even as a beginner. Your observations might make the next sprint much better. The only bad suggestion is the one you keep to yourself.

::: tip Key takeaway
A design sprint is a five-day, time-boxed process (understand, ideate, decide, prototype, test) for solving one important challenge quickly. It needs careful planning and a clear sprint brief, and it ends with a retrospective that helps the team improve.
:::
