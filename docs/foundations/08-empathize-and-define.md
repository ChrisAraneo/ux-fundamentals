# Empathize and define

This page covers the first two phases of design thinking. In the **empathize** phase, you learn about your users. In the **define** phase, you turn what you learned into a clear description of the problem to solve.

## Phase 1: Empathize

### What is empathy?

**Empathy** is the ability to understand someone else's feelings and thoughts in their situation. In UX, empathizing means trying to understand users **on their own terms**, without bias or ready-made assumptions. You look at users not only as customers, but as people with their own stories, habits, and difficulties.

The most important rule of this phase is to **set your own assumptions aside** and let research guide your decisions. Business requirements often include some information about users, but it may be incomplete or based on guesses. The empathize phase is your chance to check it, correct it, and expand it.

Common research activities in this phase include interviews, surveys, observation, and looking at competitors' products to see how users already solve their problems.

### Interviews

Interviews are one of the best ways to learn about users. UX designers most often use four formats: written **questionnaires or surveys**, **in-person interviews**, **phone interviews**, and **video interviews**.

Before you start interviewing, plan carefully:

1. **Decide what you need to know** - what kind of information you need and how much of it.
2. **Check your resources** - how much time, money, and help you have, based on the business requirements.
3. **Choose the right balance** - pick the number and format of interviews that give you enough information within the project's limits.

### Empathy maps

After interviews, you have a lot of notes. An **empathy map** helps you organize them. A classic empathy map answers five questions about a user:

1. **Who are they?** - who exactly the user is and what their situation looks like.
2. **What do they say?** - what users say out loud about the product or similar products.
3. **What do they think?** - what users think about the experience, even if they don't say it.
4. **What do they do?** - what users do before, during, and after using the product.
5. **What do they feel?** - which emotions the experience brings up, such as stress, joy, or frustration.

*Library example:* the library believed its members were mostly students and retirees. Interviews tell a different story. Many members are **shift workers and parents** who can't come during opening hours. They say "I never make it before closing time". They think the library "isn't really for people like me anymore". They borrow fewer books each year, and they feel guilty about missing reservations. The research has updated the user information in the business requirements.

### Personas

A **persona** is a fictional character who represents a group of similar users. Personas turn large amounts of research into something easy to remember and talk about. Instead of saying "some members work irregular hours", you can ask, "Would this work for Leo?"

*Library example:* the designer creates two personas.

- **Leo, 34, night-shift nurse** - finishes work at 7:00, two hours before the library opens. He loves crime novels, reads during breaks, and uses his phone for almost everything. After a shift he is tired and wants things to be quick.
- **Irena, 71, retired teacher** - visits the library every week and enjoys chatting with the librarians. She uses a tablet with very large text and is worried that "everything going online" will make the library less personal.

Personas also help you **explain your design decisions** to clients and stakeholders, because a story about a person is easier to understand than a table of research data.

## Phase 2: Define

In the define phase, you analyze your research and answer one key question: **Which user needs or problems are the most important for my design to solve?** Three tools help with this: user stories, user journeys, and problem statements.

### User stories

A **user story** is a single sentence written from the persona's point of view. It names the type of user, the action they want to take, and the reason behind it. A common template is:

> As a *type of user*, I want to *action* so that *benefit*.

*Library example:*

> As a shift worker who can't visit during opening hours, I want to reserve books online and pick them up at any time, so that reading can stay part of my routine.

User stories make a useful checklist that you can come back to when you generate and evaluate solutions.

### User journeys

A **user journey** is the series of steps and experiences a user goes through while trying to reach a goal. It can describe how the user deals with the problem today, how they use a competitor's product, or how they will use your future design.

*Library example:* Leo's current journey looks like this:

1. He finds a book in the online catalog during a night break.
2. He has to wait until morning to call the library, because reservations are only possible by phone.
3. After his shift, he waits two hours for the library to open, or he gives up and comes on his day off.
4. Sometimes the book is no longer available because his phone reservation was lost.

The designer also maps a second journey showing how Leo **would** reserve and collect a book with the new online service and locker. Comparing both journeys shows exactly where the pain points are.

### Problem statements

A **problem statement** sums up the user, their need, and the reason the need matters. It is similar to a user story, but it is written from the **designer's** perspective and usually contains more detail. A simple template is:

> *Persona name* is a *type of user* who needs *a type of experience* because *benefit of that experience*.

*Library example:*

> Leo is a night-shift nurse who needs a way to reserve and collect books outside opening hours because his work schedule makes regular visits impossible and he wants reading to remain part of his life.

Write a problem statement for **each persona**. This way, the design doesn't serve only one type of user. Irena's problem statement, for example, would focus on large, readable text and on keeping a personal connection with the librarians.

::: tip Key takeaway
In the empathize phase, you learn about your users through interviews, empathy maps, and personas. In the define phase, you turn that knowledge into user stories, user journeys, and problem statements that clearly describe the most important problems to solve.
:::
