# Ideate

## What is ideation?

**Ideation** is the process of generating many ideas on a topic **without judging them**. In the ideate phase of design thinking, you take the problem you defined in the previous phase and look for as many ways to solve it as possible. Unusual or "crazy" ideas are welcome, because they often lead to great ones.

The ideate phase has two parts:

1. **Generate** - collect as many ideas as you can. Don't criticize or choose yet, and don't settle for your first idea, because the first idea is rarely the best one.
2. **Evaluate** - when there are plenty of ideas on the table, compare them and choose the best ones to prototype. You can go back to your research or create user flows to help you decide.

## Ideation techniques

There are many techniques for generating ideas. Experienced designers choose, adapt, and combine them as needed. Here are four of the most common ones.

### "How might we...?"

In this exercise, you turn a problem statement into a list of questions that start with **"How might we..."** (HMW). HMW questions turn problems into opportunities and invite many possible answers.

*Library example:* based on Leo's and Irena's problem statements, the designer writes these questions:

- How might we let members collect books when the library is closed?
- How might we make reserving a book on a phone take less than a minute?
- How might we keep the personal touch that members like Irena value?

Then the team answers each question with as many ideas as possible.

### Crazy Eights (rapid sketching)

**Crazy Eights** is a fast, visual technique. It is especially useful when you need ideas for the look and layout of a screen. First, choose the problem or idea you want to explore. Then follow these steps:

1. **Fold a sheet of paper** - fold or divide a large sheet into eight squares.
2. **Prepare a pen** - use a thick marker so you can't get lost in details.
3. **Set a timer** - set it for eight minutes.
4. **Sketch** - draw one idea in each square, spending about one minute on each.

When the timer rings, you have eight different sketches. You can adapt the exercise, for example to four squares and four minutes when time is short.

*Library example:* the designer sketches eight versions of the "Your book is ready" screen, showing the locker number and pickup code in different ways.

### Competitive audit

A **competitive audit** is a review of how competitors solve similar problems. Ask questions such as: What products do they offer? What do those products do well? What could be better?

A competitive audit helps you find inspiration, spot gaps in the market, and learn which solutions have already worked or failed. This saves time and money, because you learn from others' experience.

*Library example:* the designer looks at other libraries' websites, bookstore apps, and parcel locker apps from delivery companies, which already solve the "pick up at any time" problem very well.

### SCAMPER

**SCAMPER** is a technique for changing and improving existing ideas. Each letter stands for an action you can apply to a design:

- **S, substitute** - replace one element with another.
- **C, combine** - merge two elements or ideas into one.
- **A, adapt** - borrow a solution from a different context.
- **M, modify** - change the size, shape, or other attributes of an element.
- **P, put to another use** - use something for a different purpose.
- **E, eliminate** - remove elements that aren't needed.
- **R, rearrange** - change the order or layout of elements.

*Library example:* the team adapts (A) the parcel locker pattern from delivery companies, combines (C) the reservation confirmation with the locker code, puts the locker to another use (P) by also accepting returns, and eliminates (E) the need to log in just to check if a book is available.

## Ideation works best together

Ideas are usually better when people with different perspectives work on them together. Design teams often run joint ideation sessions and invite stakeholders and even users.

### Questions to answer before a session

Before you organize a collaborative session, answer these questions:

- **Who** - who will take part, and what will each person's role be?
- **Where** - will the session happen in person or online?
- **Why** - what is the goal, and what should the session produce?
- **How** - which tools, methods, and rules will you use?

## Checking ideas against business requirements

Ideation starts with endless possibilities, but every project has limits. When you evaluate your ideas, go back to the business requirements and ask: Which ideas fit the budget and schedule? Which ideas meet the required standards?

Try to see these limits in a positive way. They are **guardrails** that keep the project on track and **filters** that help you choose from a long list of ideas. Limits often inspire more creative solutions, not fewer.

*Library example:* a mobile app with push notifications would be great, but it doesn't fit the budget. The team chooses a mobile-friendly website that sends a text message with the locker code. Irena's need for a personal touch is solved by adding a "Librarian's tip" to each reservation confirmation and keeping phone reservations available.

::: tip Key takeaway
Ideation means generating many ideas first and judging them later. Use techniques like "How might we", Crazy Eights, competitive audits, and SCAMPER, work together with others, and finally check your best ideas against the business requirements.
:::
