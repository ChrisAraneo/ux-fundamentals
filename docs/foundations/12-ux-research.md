# UX research

## What is UX research?

**UX research** means observing users and listening to their feedback to understand what they do, what they need, and why. Its main goal is to **make sure users stay the focus** of the whole design process, while also making sure the business goals are met.

The first rule of UX is that the user comes first. Research helps you replace guesses with facts. It shows the difference between what a team *assumes* about users and what users *really* need, and that prevents expensive mistakes.

### "You are not the user"

As a beginner, it's tempting to design based on your own taste and experience. But you are not your users: you know the product too well, and you may have completely different habits, skills, and needs. **Always back up your opinions with research**, and collect feedback before, during, and after designing.

### Useful qualities for research

Good UX researchers share three qualities:

- **Empathy** - the ability to understand other people's perspectives and feelings.
- **Pragmatism** - a practical approach to solving problems within real limits like time and budget.
- **Collaboration** - the ability to work well with designers, engineers, and stakeholders.

Becoming a full-time UX researcher requires special training, but research skills make any UX designer stronger.

## Research throughout the product life cycle

Research is not done only once. It happens at three key moments of the [product development life cycle](./03-product-development-life-cycle).

### 1. Foundational research (before design)

**Foundational research** answers the question **"What should we build?"** It happens at the very beginning, during the brainstorm stage, and helps you understand users' needs and problems, define the problem to solve, and find new opportunities. This is also when you create personas and user stories. Strong foundational research may prove your first idea wrong, and that's a good thing.

Typical questions are: What are the users' problems? How could we solve them? Am I aware of my own biases?

Common methods for foundational research include:

- **Interviews** - one-on-one conversations that collect in-depth information about people's opinions, experiences, and feelings.
- **Surveys** - the same set of questions asked to many people to learn what most of them think.
- **Focus groups** - a moderated discussion with a small group of people (for example, six to eight users) about a specific topic.
- **Competitive audits** - an overview of competitors' strengths and weaknesses.
- **Field studies** - research done in the user's own environment, such as their home or workplace, instead of in an office or lab.
- **Diary studies** - participants record their activities, behaviors, and feelings over a period of time, for example by writing short notes every day for two weeks.

### 2. Design research (during design)

**Design research** answers the question **"How should we build it?"** It happens during the design stage. Every time you create a new version of the design, research shows what works and what needs to change. Typical questions for users are: How was your experience with the prototype? What was easy or hard, and why? Did anything get in your way?

The most common method is a **usability study**: people try to complete tasks with your prototype while you observe them. The goal is to find pain points and fix them before launch. Other methods include:

- **A/B testing** - comparing two versions of a design (for example, two home page layouts) to see which one works better.
- **Guerrilla testing** - taking a prototype to a public place, such as a café, and asking passersby for a few minutes of feedback.
- **Card sorting** - participants sort cards with topic names into groups that make sense to them. It is mainly used to plan information architecture.
- **Intercepts** - short, on-the-spot conversations with people while they are doing the activity you are studying, for example asking shoppers about self-checkout right after they use it.

### 3. Post-launch research (after launch)

**Post-launch research** answers the question **"Did we succeed?"** It happens after the product is released and checks whether it meets users' needs, based on agreed measures such as adoption, usage, and user satisfaction. Common methods are **A/B tests**, **usability studies**, **surveys**, and **log analysis**, which means studying data or recordings of how people actually use the product.

Post-launch studies often use **key performance indicators (KPIs)**, which are measurable signs of progress toward a goal. Examples are the time it takes to complete a task or the number of clicks needed to make a purchase.

## Types of research methods

Research methods can be grouped in two ways: by **who** collects the data and by **what kind** of data is collected.

### Primary vs. secondary research

- **Primary research** - research you do yourself, such as interviews, surveys, or usability studies. It gives you information directly from your users.
- **Secondary research** - research that uses information collected by someone else, such as books, articles, industry reports, or public statistics. It is fast and cheap and is often done at the very beginning of a project, frequently by product leads. However, it doesn't tell you anything specific about *your* users.

### Quantitative vs. qualitative research

- **Quantitative research** - collects data that can be counted or measured, often from large groups of people through surveys with numeric answers. It answers questions like "How many?" and "How much?".
- **Qualitative research** - collects observations and stories, often through interviews with a smaller number of people. It answers questions like "Why?" and "How did this happen?".

An easy way to remember the difference: **quantitative research tells you "what" is happening, and qualitative research tells you "why"**.

## Comparing popular methods

| Method | Benefits | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Secondary research** | Fast and inexpensive; good background knowledge | Not specific to your users |
| **Interviews** | Rich, detailed insights; you can ask follow-up questions | Time-consuming and expensive; only a few participants |
| **Surveys** | Cheap and quick; reach many people | Little depth; answers are limited to the options you provide |
| **Usability studies** | You see first-hand how people use the design | Can be expensive; a test setting is not the same as real life |

### Open-ended questions

Interviews work best with **open-ended questions**, which can't be answered with a simple "yes", "no", or number. Compare these two questions:

- "On a scale of 1 to 10, how would you rate the app?"
- "Tell me about the last time you used the app."

The first question gives you a number. The second question gives you a story, which is usually much more useful for understanding the user.

## Choosing the right method

There is no single best method. **The method depends on the question you need to answer.** Define your research questions first, and the right method will follow:

- **Background knowledge** - use secondary research when you need existing data or context.
- **Understanding behavior and motivation** - use interviews and usability studies when you need to learn why people act the way they do.
- **Measuring at scale** - use surveys when you need to confirm how common a problem is across many users.

::: tip Key takeaway
UX research keeps the user at the center. Use foundational research to decide what to build, design research to decide how to build it, and post-launch research to check whether you succeeded. Choose methods based on your research questions.
:::
